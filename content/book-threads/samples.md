## Mark 1:1-8
*Mark · section 1 of 87 · key verse Mark 1:7-8*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in the prophets, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you: the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!’ ” John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins. All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to him. They were baptized by him in the Jordan river, confessing their sins. John was clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist. He ate locusts and wild honey. He preached, saying, “After me comes he who is mightier than I, the thong of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen. I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

</details>

**Thought**

Mark gets straight to the point. His first line says what the whole book is about: the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. Then, before Jesus appears, Mark reaches back to the prophets. God promised to send a messenger ahead of him, a voice in the wilderness calling people to prepare the way of the Lord. That voice is John. Out in the wilderness he preaches a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. People come out to him from Jerusalem and the Judean countryside, and he baptizes them in the Jordan as they confess their sins. Mark pauses on his rough clothes of camel's hair and leather and his food of locusts and wild honey.

Then John speaks of someone else. He says that one more powerful is coming after him, and that he is not worthy to stoop down and untie that man's sandal straps, which was a servant's job. John baptizes with water. The one coming will baptize with the Holy Spirit. The crowds came out to John, and John points them past himself.

**Illustration**

Imagine a traveler crossing dry country who comes to a sign pointing toward the town ahead. The sign might be weathered and plain, or odd enough to make the traveler stop and stare. But a sign that kept people standing in front of it, admiring it, would have failed at the one thing it is for. What makes it worth anything is the direction it faces and the distance it points across. John draws crowds into the wilderness, and they listen, confess, and step into the river. He could have let them stay with him. Instead he turns them to face down the road toward someone greater, someone who brings not water on the skin but God's own Spirit. The sign is honest about being only a sign, and that honesty is what makes it trustworthy.

**Ponder**

- Why might Mark open his good news with John in the wilderness instead of starting with Jesus himself?
- What do you notice about John counting himself unworthy even to untie the sandals of the one coming?
- What would it mean for the Holy Spirit, not just water, to touch your life?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for keeping his word through the prophets and sending a messenger to prepare the way.
- Confess your sins honestly, as the people at the Jordan did, and ask for God's forgiveness.
- Ask to be filled with the Holy Spirit by the one John said was mightier than himself.

## Psalms 1:1-6
*Psalms · section 1 of 171 · key verse Psalms 1:2-3*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Blessed is the man who doesn’t walk in the counsel of the wicked, nor stand on the path of sinners, nor sit in the seat of scoffers; but his delight is in Yahweh’s law. On his law he meditates day and night. He will be like a tree planted by the streams of water, that produces its fruit in its season, whose leaf also does not wither. Whatever he does shall prosper. The wicked are not so, but are like the chaff which the wind drives away. Therefore the wicked shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous. For Yahweh knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked shall perish.

</details>

**Thought**

Psalm 1 stands at the entrance to the Psalter and sets two ways of living side by side. It describes the blessed person first by what he refuses: he does not take the advice of the wicked, linger on the path of sinners, or settle among mockers. Walking, standing, sitting: the picture is of drifting into a place and then staying there. Instead he delights in God's law and ponders it day and night. He is like a tree beside streams of water, bearing fruit at its proper time, its leaves green. The wicked are like chaff, light husks the wind carries off. They will not stand in the judgment or in the gathering of the righteous. The psalm ends with God himself, who knows and watches over the way of the righteous, while the way of the wicked perishes.

Slow down on verses 2 and 3. Delight in God's instruction, turned over again and again, makes a person like a planted tree. Notice that the tree does not make the stream. Someone planted it there, and it lives on water it did not supply. Its fruit comes when it is ready.

**Illustration**

Picture a dry landscape after a long stretch without rain. The grass on the hills has gone brown and brittle. Yet if you follow the course of a river, you can trace it from far off by a ribbon of green, where willows and other trees stand in full leaf. They are no tougher than the trees on the hillside. The difference is underground, where their roots have reached water that nobody passing by can see. Their flourishing is not hurried or showy. They stay where they were planted and keep drinking. The psalm holds up this kind of picture for a life rooted in delight in God's instruction. It is not a life spared from heat. It is a life drawing on a hidden source while the heat goes on.

**Ponder**

- What counsel or company do you notice yourself drifting toward, first walking, then standing, then sitting?
- What would it look like for God's instruction to be a delight to you rather than only a duty?
- Where are your roots reaching, beneath what other people can see?

**Prayer**

- Ask God to grow in you a real delight in his word.
- Thank God that he knows and watches over the way of the righteous.
- Bring to God any place where you have settled among voices that pull you away from him.

## Ruth 1:1-5
*Ruth · section 1 of 9 · key verse Ruth 1:5*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. A certain man of Bethlehem Judah went to live in the country of Moab with his wife and his two sons. The name of the man was Elimelech, and the name of his wife Naomi. The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem Judah. They came into the country of Moab and lived there. Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left with her two sons. They took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. Mahlon and Chilion both died, and the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband.

</details>

**Thought**

Ruth opens in the time of the judges, with famine in the land. Elimelech of Bethlehem in Judah takes his wife Naomi and their sons, Mahlon and Chilion, across the border to live in Moab. Many readers notice the irony here, because Bethlehem's name is often taken to mean house of bread. Then the losses come in short, flat sentences. Elimelech dies. The sons marry two Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, who are living in their own homeland. About ten years pass, and both sons die. When these verses end, three widows are left in Moab, though the narrator keeps watching Naomi.

Verse 5 says it simply: Naomi is left without her two sons and without her husband. The text gives no explanation, lays no blame and offers no quick comfort. It just records the emptiness. In her world, a woman with no husband or sons faced grief and real insecurity as well. God is not mentioned in these verses at all. The story starts from this bare place, and everything that follows in the book grows out of it.

**Illustration**

Think of a kitchen table in a family home. Once there were four chairs, and all four were filled every evening. Then one stays empty, and the others learn not to look at it. Later two new faces pull up chairs, and the table feels full again. Then two more chairs go empty. The woman who has sat there longest now looks across at two young women who are grieving too, and the room has never been this quiet. No words could fill those places. The story of Ruth begins at a table like that, and it doesn't hurry anyone away from it. Before anything is mended, the narrator counts the empty places one by one, so the reader feels how much is gone.

**Ponder**

- Why might the story name each loss so plainly before anything else happens?
- What would it be like to be Orpah or Ruth here, widowed in your own homeland?
- How do you look for God in a passage, or a life, where he is not named?

**Prayer**

- Bring before God people who are grieving, especially anyone who feels left alone after loss.
- Ask God for the honesty to name your losses to him without hurrying past them.
- Pray for families who leave their homes because of hunger and need.

## Philippians 1:1-2
*Philippians · section 1 of 12 · key verse Philippians 1:1*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Paul and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ; To all the saints in Christ Jesus who are at Philippi, with the overseers and servants: Grace to you, and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

</details>

**Thought**

The letter opens the way letters in Paul's world usually opened: who is writing, to whom, and a greeting. Yet each part has been reshaped. Paul names Timothy beside him and gives neither of them a title of rank. Both are servants, a word for someone who belongs to another. The letter is addressed to every one of the saints in Christ Jesus at Philippi, and their overseers and deacons are named alongside them. Then the usual greeting becomes a blessing: grace and peace, coming from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together. Hints of the letter's later concern with humility and partnership already show here.

Slow down on the first verse. The believers have two addresses. They live at Philippi, a real city with its streets, work and neighbours. They also live in Christ Jesus. The word saints does not mean the especially accomplished. It means those set apart as God's own. Paul uses it for the whole congregation, leaders and everyone else. Notice that being in Christ does not lift them out of Philippi. They are holy right there in Philippi.

**Illustration**

Think of an address written on an envelope. It names a person, then narrows to a street, a town and a country, so the letter can find its way to one particular door. Paul's opening gives the believers in Philippi an address like that, with one extra line. They are at Philippi, and they are in Christ Jesus. Now imagine a nurse working a night shift, a shopkeeper counting the till, a parent clearing the table. Their surroundings are ordinary and fixed: the same ward, the same shop, the same kitchen. Paul's greeting suggests that people like these, belonging to Christ, carry a second address into each of those places. The ward is no less real, and neither is their belonging to God. The letter does not ask the Philippians to move house before they can be saints. It finds them where they already live and calls them holy there.

**Ponder**

- What changes when you think of your ordinary surroundings as the place where you are also in Christ?
- Why might Paul introduce himself and Timothy simply as servants rather than by any title?
- How does it sit with you to be counted among God's saints?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for the grace and peace that come from the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.
- Ask to belong to Christ as fully in your ordinary place as anywhere else.
- Pray for those who lead and serve in churches you know, and for every member alongside them.

*Fixed in 1 fix-up round(s). Problems sent back:*
- The claim that "Much of what the letter goes on to say about humility, joy and partnership is already here in seed" reads into the opening something it doesn't contain: servanthood suggests humility and the joint address suggests partnership, but nothing in verses 1-2 points to joy.

## Leviticus 1:1-17
*Leviticus · section 1 of 81 · key verse Leviticus 1:4*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Yahweh called to Moses, and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting, saying, “Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When anyone of you offers an offering to Yahweh, you shall offer your offering of the livestock, from the herd and from the flock. “ ‘If his offering is a burnt offering from the herd, he shall offer a male without defect. He shall offer it at the door of the Tent of Meeting, that he may be accepted before Yahweh. He shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him. He shall kill the bull before Yahweh. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall present the blood and sprinkle the blood around on the altar that is at the door of the Tent of Meeting. He shall skin the burnt offering, and cut it into pieces. The sons of Aaron the priest shall put fire on the altar, and lay wood in order on the fire; and Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall lay the pieces, the head, and the fat in order on the wood that is on the fire which is on the altar; but he shall wash its innards and its legs with water. The priest shall burn all of it on the altar, for a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh. “ ‘If his offering is from the flock, from the sheep or from the goats, for a burnt offering, he shall offer a male without defect. He shall kill it on the north side of the altar before Yahweh. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall sprinkle its blood around on the altar. He shall cut it into its pieces, with its head and its fat. The priest shall lay them in order on the wood that is on the fire which is on the altar, but the innards and the legs he shall wash with water. The priest shall offer the whole, and burn it on the altar. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh. “ ‘If his offering to Yahweh is a burnt offering of birds, then he shall offer his offering from turtledoves or of young pigeons. The priest shall bring it to the altar, and wring off its head, and burn it on the altar; and its blood shall be drained out on the side of the altar; and he shall take away its crop and its feathers, and cast it beside the altar on the east part, in the place of the ashes. He shall tear it by its wings, but shall not divide it apart. The priest shall burn it on the altar, on the wood that is on the fire. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh.

</details>

**Thought**

Leviticus opens with the LORD calling to Moses from the Tent of Meeting. His first words are about how a person may come near him with an offering. The chapter sets out the burnt offering in three forms. If the offering comes from the herd or the flock, a bull or a sheep or goat, the worshipper brings an unblemished male. He kills it and cuts it up, while Aaron's sons the priests handle the blood, lay the fire and arrange the pieces. The priest burns the whole animal. If the offering is a turtledove or young pigeon, the priest brings it to the altar himself. He throws its crop aside in the place of the ashes and burns the bird. Each time the passage ends the same way: it is a fragrance that pleases the LORD. A bull and a pigeon close with identical words.

In verse 4 the person bringing the offering lays a hand on the animal's head, and God accepts the animal on that person's behalf, to make atonement for them. The gesture binds worshipper and offering together. Many Christians have also seen here a picture of Christ giving himself for others.

**Illustration**

Imagine a man standing at the entrance of the tent with a young bull from his own herd. He has looked it over carefully, and it has no flaw. It is worth a great deal to him. The animal is warm under his palm, and he can feel it breathing as he presses his hand onto its head. He says nothing aloud, but the hand speaks for him: this one is mine, and it stands here for me. Then he kills it himself, with his own hands. He doesn't watch from a distance while someone else does it. Think how differently he walks home than if he had just paid a fee at the door. He has felt the cost of coming near a holy God, and he has heard that God accepts the offering.

**Ponder**

- What might it mean that a small bird and a costly bull both end with the same words of God's pleasure?
- Why might the worshipper be asked to lay their own hand on the offering instead of simply handing it over?
- What does it stir in you that the first word from the tent is about how to come near?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that he opens a way for people to come near him and be accepted.
- Ask for a deeper awareness of both the holiness of God and the cost of approaching him.
- Offer thanks for Christ, whom many Christians see as giving himself wholly on behalf of others.

*Fixed in 1 fix-up round(s). Problems sent back:*
- The thought lists all three forms and then says "The worshipper brings a flawless male to the entrance of the tent" as if it applied to every one of them. The section only asks for an unblemished male from the herd or the flock. For birds it names a turtledove or young pigeon with no sex or flawlessness required, and the priest brings the bird to the altar.
- The phrase "Each time the priest burns all of it on the altar" uses an absolute word the bird instructions do not support. There the priest takes away the crop (and the feathers in WEB and KJV) and throws it beside the altar in the place of the ashes. Only for the larger animals does the text say all or the whole is burned.

## Judges 19:16-30
*Judges · section 41 of 46 · key verse Judges 19:30*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Behold, an old man came from his work out of the field at evening. Now the man was from the hill country of Ephraim, and he lived in Gibeah; but the men of the place were Benjamites. He lifted up his eyes, and saw the wayfaring man in the street of the city; and the old man said, “Where are you going? Where did you come from?” He said to him, “We are passing from Bethlehem Judah to the farther side of the hill country of Ephraim. I am from there, and I went to Bethlehem Judah. I am going to Yahweh’s house; and there is no one who has taken me into his house. Yet there is both straw and feed for our donkeys; and there is bread and wine also for me, and for your servant, and for the young man who is with your servants. There is no lack of anything.” The old man said, “Peace be to you! Just let me supply all your needs, but don’t sleep in the street.” So he brought him into his house, and gave the donkeys fodder. Then they washed their feet, and ate and drank. As they were making their hearts merry, behold, the men of the city, certain wicked fellows, surrounded the house, beating at the door; and they spoke to the master of the house, the old man, saying, “Bring out the man who came into your house, that we can have sex with him!” The man, the master of the house, went out to them, and said to them, “No, my brothers, please don’t act so wickedly; since this man has come into my house, don’t do this folly. Behold, here is my virgin daughter and his concubine. I will bring them out now. Humble them, and do with them what seems good to you; but to this man don’t do any such folly.” But the men wouldn’t listen to him; so the man grabbed his concubine, and brought her out to them; and they had sex with her, and abused her all night until the morning. When the day began to dawn, they let her go. Then the woman came in the dawning of the day, and fell down at the door of the man’s house where her lord was, until it was light. Her lord rose up in the morning and opened the doors of the house, and went out to go his way; and behold, the woman his concubine had fallen down at the door of the house, with her hands on the threshold. He said to her, “Get up, and let’s get going!” but no one answered. Then he took her up on the donkey; and the man rose up, and went to his place. When he had come into his house, he took a knife and cut up his concubine, and divided her, limb by limb, into twelve pieces, and sent her throughout all the borders of Israel. It was so, that all who saw it said, “Such a deed has not been done or seen from the day that the children of Israel came up out of the land of Egypt to this day! Consider it, take counsel, and speak.”

</details>

**Thought**

Evening falls in Gibeah, a Benjamite town, and no one takes the travelers in until an old man from Ephraim opens his home. Earlier in the chapter the Levite passed by Jebus, a foreign city, to lodge among Israelites; now Israelite men surround the house and demand the guest. The host refuses, yet offers his own daughter and the Levite's concubine. When the men will not listen, the Levite takes his concubine and brings her out to them. They rape and abuse her through the night. At dawn she falls at the door, her hands on the threshold; when her master tells her to get up, no answer comes. He carries her home, cuts her body into twelve pieces and sends them through Israel. Judges has traced a long decline, and here it reaches Israel's own people. In verse 30 all who see it say nothing like this has happened since Israel came out of Egypt, and they call on Israel to consider, take counsel and speak. The text adds no lesson to soften it; it asks Israel to look, to weigh it, and not stay silent.

**Illustration**

Think about how a community often responds when something unspeakable happens among its own people. People feel a pull to look away, to change the subject, to let the matter close quietly, because naming it would mean admitting what the place has become. Now imagine a town meeting where someone stands and says that no one leaves until they have looked at what happened. Nobody in that room is comfortable. But that discomfort is better than silence. The words at the end of this chapter work like that. Notice the order: consider first, then take counsel, then speak. Notice too that the woman herself never says a word anywhere in the chapter. Her last act is to reach the door of the house where her master was. The call to speak falls on everyone who saw, and the text sets that same call before everyone who reads.

**Ponder**

- Who in this scene could have protected the woman, and what does each of them choose instead?
- Why might the text show her hands on the threshold without adding any comment?
- What makes it hard to consider, take counsel and speak when harm is done among God's own people?

**Prayer**

- Pray for people who have suffered violence and abuse, especially where the people meant to protect them failed them.
- Ask God for the courage to look honestly at evil and not turn away from it.
- Pray for communities of faith to listen to those who have been harmed and to speak truthfully.

- The thought is almost entirely a retelling of events. It never slows down on the key verse (19:30) to say what it says and then reflect on it; one closing sentence ("That is the text's own response...") is all it gets. It also says little about why the section matters in Judges beyond one mention of an earlier scene in the chapter.
- "the Levite seizes his concubine and pushes her out to them" adds a detail none of the three translations give: they say he took or grabbed her and brought or sent her out, not that he pushed her.
- The thought says "The Levite had passed by a foreign city to trust Israelites" and later calls Gibeah "the town the Levite thought safe". Both come from earlier in Judges 19 (the stop at Jebus), not from this section, and the reading presents them as if they were part of this section instead of saying they happen earlier in the chapter.

## 1 Chronicles 1:1-4
*1 Chronicles · section 1 of 103 · key verse 1 Chronicles 1:1*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

</details>

**Thought**

The book opens without a story, a speaker or a setting. It opens with names, thirteen of them, set down one after another: from Adam down to Noah, and then Shem, Ham and Japheth, whom some translations name as Noah's sons. No one acts, no one speaks, and the writer explains nothing. He expects his readers to know these names, or at least to see that they are worth writing down. Chronicles will go on to trace families through the nations and the tribes of Israel toward David and the house of God in Jerusalem. But it does not begin with Israel. It begins with the first human being, and by the end of this short list it has spread out into three brothers.

Slow down on the first verse. It is only three names: Adam, then Seth (Sheth in some translations), then Enosh. There is no verb, no title and no comment. Before the book says a word about kings, priests or temple, it places the whole story inside the human family. Even a bare name, set in its place, quietly says that this person counted.

**Illustration**

Think of a family Bible passed down from one household to the next, with names written on its front pages in different hands and different inks. Whoever opens it now may know nothing about most of these people: not their faces, not their work, not their troubles. Still, nobody tears those pages out. The names are there because someone thought each life worth recording, and because the person holding the book belongs to that line, whether they knew those people or not. Opening Chronicles with Adam, Seth and Enosh works something like that. Before any of the famous names arrive, the writer writes in the first names on the page, and that makes room for every name that follows.

**Ponder**

- Why might a book so concerned with Israel, David and the temple begin with Adam?
- What changes when you see your own life as part of a human family that begins with Adam?
- Whose name, known to few, would you want remembered before God?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that the story this book tells opens with the whole human family.
- Bring before God people whose names few others remember, and entrust them to his care.
- Ask God for humility and gratitude to take your own small place among the generations.

## Song of Solomon 1:1-4
*Song of Solomon · section 1 of 14 · key verse Song of Solomon 1:3*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your love is better than wine. Your oils have a pleasing fragrance. Your name is oil poured out, therefore the virgins love you. Take me away with you. Let’s hurry. The king has brought me into his rooms. We will be glad and rejoice in you. We will praise your love more than wine! They are right to love you.

</details>

**Thought**

The book names itself the Song of Songs, a Hebrew way of saying the finest song, and links it with Solomon. Then, without any introduction, a woman speaks. She longs for his kisses, and in a single breath she turns from speaking about him to speaking to him. His love is better than wine. His perfumes are lovely. His very name is like perfume poured out, and that is why the young women love him. She asks him to take her away, quickly. The translations differ over whether the king has already brought her into his rooms or whether she is wishing he would. Then other voices join her. They delight in him, praise his love above wine and agree that he is rightly loved.

Stay with verse 3. Here a name stands for the whole person, his character and his reputation. Perfume that has been poured out does not stay in the jar. It fills the room. She is saying that who he is reaches the people around him. Many Christians have also heard in this a picture of God's love, while still holding that the poem first sings of a woman and a man.

**Illustration**

Imagine someone carrying a small bottle of fine perfume into a quiet house. While it stays sealed, nobody notices it. Once it is opened and poured, the scent travels by itself: down the hallway, into the next room, into clothes and curtains. People who never saw the bottle know it is there. Now think of a person whose name works a little like that. Someone mentions them, and before any details come, something in you warms because of who they are and how they treat people. That is the kind of thing the woman is saying. His name is not just a label stuck on him. It carries who he is, and it spreads. The poem gives this as the reason the young women love him: the fragrance of who he is has reached them.

**Ponder**

- What might it mean that Scripture opens this book with a woman voicing her longing openly and without apology?
- Whose name brings you warmth when you hear it, simply because of who they are?
- In your life with God, when do you speak about him, and when do you speak to him?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for the gift of love and desire between a woman and a man, celebrated openly here in Scripture.
- Ask that your life might carry something good to the people near you, the way poured perfume fills a room.
- Stop speaking about God and speak to him, telling him plainly what you love in him.
