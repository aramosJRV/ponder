## Mark 1:1-8
*Mark · section 1 of 87 · key verse Mark 1:7-8*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in the prophets, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you: the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!’ ” John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins. All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to him. They were baptized by him in the Jordan river, confessing their sins. John was clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist. He ate locusts and wild honey. He preached, saying, “After me comes he who is mightier than I, the thong of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen. I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

</details>

**Thought**

Mark wastes no breath. There is no birth story here and no family tree. There is only a heading: this is where the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God, begins. Then, before Jesus appears, Mark reaches back to Israel's prophets. They spoke of a messenger sent ahead, and of a voice in the wilderness calling people to clear a road for the Lord. The good news opens with an old promise, not a new idea.

John is that voice. He appears in the wilderness wearing camel's hair and a leather belt, and he eats locusts and wild honey. He preaches a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. People come out to the Jordan from the Judean countryside and from Jerusalem, confessing their sins.

Yet everything John says points away from himself. Someone mightier is coming after him. John counts himself unworthy even to stoop and untie that person's sandal straps, a servant's task. John baptizes with water, but the one coming will baptize with the Holy Spirit. When the section ends, Jesus has not yet appeared, and everything leans toward him.

**Illustration**

Imagine a remote village that hears a king will soon pass through. Nobody waits for him to arrive before getting ready. They fill the potholes, cut back the brush, haul stones off the track and sweep the doorsteps. The work is ordinary and a little humbling, and they do it for someone they have not yet seen. The cleared road says two things at once: this is how unready we were, and this is how much we want him to come.

John's call to repentance works something like that. People went out to the river and named their sins aloud, clearing a way in themselves. And John, the one making the announcement, never mistook himself for the king. A herald who starts to believe the crowds came to see him has forgotten his job. John never forgets. He keeps pointing down the road.

**Ponder**

- Why might Mark begin the good news with an old promise from the prophets rather than with Jesus himself?
- What might it look like for you to make the way ready for the Lord?
- How does John's lowliness before the one coming after him challenge your picture of greatness?

**Prayer**

- Ask for the honesty to name your sins before God, as the people at the Jordan did.
- Ask that the Holy Spirit, whom John promised, would be at work in you.
- Thank God that the good news rests on promises he has spoken and keeps.

## Ruth 1:1-5
*Ruth · section 1 of 9 · key verse Ruth 1:5*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. A certain man of Bethlehem Judah went to live in the country of Moab with his wife and his two sons. The name of the man was Elimelech, and the name of his wife Naomi. The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem Judah. They came into the country of Moab and lived there. Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left with her two sons. They took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. Mahlon and Chilion both died, and the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband.

</details>

**Thought**

Ruth opens in a hard place. The story is set in the days when the judges ruled, a time the book of Judges remembers as unsettled and often lawless, and there is famine in the land. A man from Bethlehem, a town whose name means house of bread, takes his wife and two sons across into Moab to find food. In five verses the story moves quickly from leaving, to settling, to burial. Elimelech dies. His sons marry two Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. About ten years pass, and then Mahlon and Chilion die as well. The narrator gives no reasons and passes no verdict on anyone's choices. The losses are simply set down, one after another. By the end Naomi has outlived her husband and both her sons. She is a widow far from her own people, with two widowed daughters-in-law beside her and no son to carry the family on. God is not named once in these verses, and that silence is worth noticing. The book begins where many lives feel they are: emptied out, with nothing visible being done.

**Illustration**

Think of a kitchen table in a rented house in another country. At first it is set for four: a father, a mother and two growing boys, glad to have bread again. Later two more chairs are pulled up as the sons bring home wives from the town around them, and the table gets louder and fuller. Then the chair at the head stays empty. Some time after that, two more chairs go empty. In the end three women sit at a table built for a bigger family. One of them is a long way from the place she grew up, and the other two are at home but suddenly unsure what home now means. Nobody has to explain what happened. The empty chairs say it. Ruth's opening lines work like that table. They count who is gone, and then they leave the three who remain sitting there.

**Ponder**

- What does it mean to you that the narrator records Naomi's losses without explaining them or blaming anyone?
- Where have you known a time when God seemed absent from the story, even though you were still in it?
- What might Orpah and Ruth, widowed in their own land, have been carrying alongside Naomi?

**Prayer**

- Pray for people facing famine or hunger who have to leave home to survive.
- Bring to God anyone you know who is grieving loss upon loss, asking him to be near them.
- Ask God for the honesty to name your own losses before him without hurrying past them.

## Philippians 1:1-2
*Philippians · section 1 of 12 · key verse Philippians 1:1-2*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Paul and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ; To all the saints in Christ Jesus who are at Philippi, with the overseers and servants: Grace to you, and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

</details>

**Thought**

In Paul's world a letter opened with the writer's name, the people it was for, and a greeting. This one keeps that shape, but every part of it is filled with Christ. Paul names Timothy beside him, and the two share one title: servants of Christ Jesus. The word behind it can also mean slaves, people who belong to someone else. Unlike several of his other letters, this one does not have Paul calling himself an apostle. He stands on level ground with Timothy, and both belong to the same Lord.

The readers have two addresses. They are at Philippi, a real city with streets and neighbours, and they are in Christ Jesus. Paul calls all of them saints. They have not climbed to some spiritual height. The name means God has set them apart as his own. The overseers and deacons are greeted too, and they are named alongside the whole church.

Then comes the blessing: grace and peace, from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ, named side by side. Before Paul says anything else, he reminds this church where grace and peace come from.

**Illustration**

Imagine an envelope with two lines of address. The first is ordinary: a house number, a street, the name of a town. A postman could find it on any map. The second line is stranger. It gives no place at all, only a name: in Christ Jesus. Both lines are true at once. The people in that house still buy bread, pay what they owe, and fall out and make up with the neighbours on that street. Yet the letter insists they also live somewhere else, in a belonging no map can show. Paul writes to the church this way. He does not take them out of Philippi to make them holy, and he does not let Philippi be the whole truth about them. Anyone opening such a letter is reminded where they stand and whose they are.

**Ponder**

- What would it be like to see yourself, as Paul and Timothy did, first as a servant of Christ Jesus?
- What would it mean to see your own town and street as the place where you are in Christ?
- Why might Paul offer grace and peace before he says anything else?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for the grace and peace that come from him as Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ.
- Pray for a church you know, both its leaders and everyone in it, by name if you can.
- Ask for a heart that is content to be called a servant of Christ Jesus.

## Judges 19:16-30
*Judges · section 41 of 46 · key verse Judges 19:30*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Behold, an old man came from his work out of the field at evening. Now the man was from the hill country of Ephraim, and he lived in Gibeah; but the men of the place were Benjamites. He lifted up his eyes, and saw the wayfaring man in the street of the city; and the old man said, “Where are you going? Where did you come from?” He said to him, “We are passing from Bethlehem Judah to the farther side of the hill country of Ephraim. I am from there, and I went to Bethlehem Judah. I am going to Yahweh’s house; and there is no one who has taken me into his house. Yet there is both straw and feed for our donkeys; and there is bread and wine also for me, and for your servant, and for the young man who is with your servants. There is no lack of anything.” The old man said, “Peace be to you! Just let me supply all your needs, but don’t sleep in the street.” So he brought him into his house, and gave the donkeys fodder. Then they washed their feet, and ate and drank. As they were making their hearts merry, behold, the men of the city, certain wicked fellows, surrounded the house, beating at the door; and they spoke to the master of the house, the old man, saying, “Bring out the man who came into your house, that we can have sex with him!” The man, the master of the house, went out to them, and said to them, “No, my brothers, please don’t act so wickedly; since this man has come into my house, don’t do this folly. Behold, here is my virgin daughter and his concubine. I will bring them out now. Humble them, and do with them what seems good to you; but to this man don’t do any such folly.” But the men wouldn’t listen to him; so the man grabbed his concubine, and brought her out to them; and they had sex with her, and abused her all night until the morning. When the day began to dawn, they let her go. Then the woman came in the dawning of the day, and fell down at the door of the man’s house where her lord was, until it was light. Her lord rose up in the morning and opened the doors of the house, and went out to go his way; and behold, the woman his concubine had fallen down at the door of the house, with her hands on the threshold. He said to her, “Get up, and let’s get going!” but no one answered. Then he took her up on the donkey; and the man rose up, and went to his place. When he had come into his house, he took a knife and cut up his concubine, and divided her, limb by limb, into twelve pieces, and sent her throughout all the borders of Israel. It was so, that all who saw it said, “Such a deed has not been done or seen from the day that the children of Israel came up out of the land of Egypt to this day! Consider it, take counsel, and speak.”

</details>

**Thought**

The section opens with what looks like rescue. An old man from Ephraim, living in Benjamite Gibeah, will not let the travelers sleep in the square. He feeds the donkeys, and his guests wash and eat. Then wicked men of the town surround the house and demand the guest. The scene echoes Lot's house in Sodom, but here it is an Israelite town, and no rescue comes. The host offers his virgin daughter and the concubine. The Levite seizes his concubine and puts her out to them, and they rape and abuse her all night. At dawn she falls at the door, her hands on the threshold. Her master opens it to leave, tells her to get up, and gets no answer; the text never says when she died. He carries her home, cuts her into twelve pieces and sends them throughout Israel. Those who see it say nothing like it has happened since Israel came out of Egypt, and call for counsel and speech. The woman has no name and no words. Apart from calling the men wicked, the narrator explains nothing; the deed is left to show how far things have fallen.

**Illustration**

Think about what a front door is meant to be. In the world of this story, crossing someone's threshold changed things. The person outside became a guest, and the household took on the duty of keeping them safe. The old man in Gibeah knows this. It is why he will not let the traveler spend the night in the square. Yet in this scene the door seems to work only one way. It opens to put the woman out to the crowd, and it does not open again for her through the dark hours. In the morning she lies there with her hands on the threshold, the very line that was supposed to mean protection. The door opens again only because her master is ready to leave. The section quietly asks the reader to notice who that door protected, and who it did not.

**Ponder**

- Why might the narrator give the woman no name and no words, yet stop to describe her hands on the threshold?
- Where in this scene does protecting some people cost others everything?
- What does it mean to you that Scripture tells a story this dark without explaining it away?

**Prayer**

- Pray for people who suffer violence and go unheard, asking God to see them and bring justice.
- Ask God for honesty to see where comfort or self-protection has made you deaf to someone else's danger.
- Pray for communities and churches to consider, take counsel and speak when terrible wrongs come to light.
