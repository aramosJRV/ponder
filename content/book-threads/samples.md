## Mark 1:1-8
*Mark · section 1 of 87 · key verse Mark 1:7-8*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. As it is written in the prophets, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you: the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!’ ” John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins. All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to him. They were baptized by him in the Jordan river, confessing their sins. John was clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist. He ate locusts and wild honey. He preached, saying, “After me comes he who is mightier than I, the thong of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen. I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

</details>

**Thought**

Mark opens without a birth story or a family tree. His first line names his subject: this is the beginning of the good news about Jesus, the Christ, the Son of God. Then he reaches back to the prophets, to words about a messenger sent ahead and a voice in the wilderness calling people to prepare the Lord's way. John is that voice. In the wilderness he baptizes and preaches a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, and people come out from the Judean countryside and from Jerusalem to be baptized in the Jordan, confessing their sins. His camel's hair, leather belt, locusts and wild honey all belong to the wilderness. Yet for all the crowds, John points away from himself. In verses 7 and 8 he says someone more powerful is coming after him, someone whose sandal straps he counts himself unworthy to stoop and untie, a task often thought of as a servant's job. John baptizes with water; the coming one will baptize with the Holy Spirit. The man drawing the crowds spends his voice on someone not yet in the scene.

**Illustration**

Imagine standing at night where a road comes down out of dark hills. A man waits at the roadside with a lantern, and people gather around him because his is the only light for miles. They want to look at him, to hear him, to stay near the glow. But he keeps lifting the lantern toward the road and telling them to watch it, because someone is coming along that road whose light will make his own look small. His lantern is real, and it does real good: it shows people where they stand and where the road is. Still, anyone who stared only at the lantern would miss the reason it was lit. John's water and the repentance he preaches are real too, yet he speaks of them as making ready for someone greater, who will give what John himself cannot.

**Ponder**

- Why might Mark begin his account of Jesus with John pointing ahead, rather than with Jesus himself speaking?
- What might it mean for you to confess your sins as honestly as the people at the Jordan did?
- Where do you find yourself wanting to be the light people look at rather than the one pointing beyond?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that the good news begins with Jesus, the Christ, the Son of God.
- Ask for honesty to confess your sins and trust in the forgiveness John proclaimed.
- Pray for something of John's humility, glad to point to the one who baptizes with the Holy Spirit.

## Psalms 1:1-6
*Psalms · section 1 of 171 · key verse Psalms 1:2-3*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Blessed is the man who doesn’t walk in the counsel of the wicked, nor stand on the path of sinners, nor sit in the seat of scoffers; but his delight is in Yahweh’s law. On his law he meditates day and night. He will be like a tree planted by the streams of water, that produces its fruit in its season, whose leaf also does not wither. Whatever he does shall prosper. The wicked are not so, but are like the chaff which the wind drives away. Therefore the wicked shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous. For Yahweh knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked shall perish.

</details>

**Thought**

The book of Psalms opens with a picture of two ways rather than with a prayer. It pronounces a blessing on the person who does not follow the advice of the wicked, stand on the road sinners take, or sit down among mockers. Walking, then standing, then sitting: the verbs trace a slow settling in. This person delights in the Lord's law instead and ponders it day and night. They are like a well-watered tree, fruitful and green, and whatever they do prospers. The wicked are the opposite. They are chaff, light and rootless, driven off by the wind, and they will not stand in the judgment or in the assembly of the righteous. The psalm ends with the Lord himself, who knows and guards the way of the righteous, while the way of the wicked perishes.

Slow down at verses 2 and 3. This person takes joy in God's instruction and keeps turning it over. The result is a tree planted beside streams. Notice that the tree did not plant itself. Someone set it where the water is. It bears fruit when fruit is due, not all at once. Its flourishing comes from being rooted close to what gives life.

**Illustration**

Imagine looking down on a dry valley at the end of a long, hot summer. The hillsides are brown, and the grass crackles underfoot. Down the middle of the valley runs a line of deep green trees. You cannot see the stream from up there, but you can trace exactly where it goes by the green. Those trees are not straining harder than the scrub on the slopes. Their roots simply reach water that the hillside plants cannot. Now picture a threshing floor on the same valley floor. Someone tosses the grain into the breeze. The heavy kernels drop back down, and the husks drift off and vanish. The psalm puts these two images side by side. One is rooted and keeps bearing fruit. The other has nothing to hold it in place.

**Ponder**

- Whose counsel, which paths and which seats shape you without your quite noticing?
- What would it mean for God's instruction to be a delight to you and not simply a duty?
- What do you feel when you read that the Lord knows the way of the righteous?

**Prayer**

- Ask the Lord to root you near what gives life and to grow real delight in his instruction.
- Thank God that he knows and guards the way of those who walk with him.
- Bring before him any slow slide toward mockery that you recognise in yourself.

## Ruth 1:1-5

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. A certain man of Bethlehem Judah went to live in the country of Moab with his wife and his two sons. The name of the man was Elimelech, and the name of his wife Naomi. The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem Judah. They came into the country of Moab and lived there. Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left with her two sons. They took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years. Mahlon and Chilion both died, and the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband.

</details>

**Thought**

Ruth opens in the time when the judges ruled, with famine in the land. Elimelech, a man from Bethlehem in Judah, takes his wife Naomi and their sons Mahlon and Chilion across to Moab and settles there. Then Elimelech dies. The sons marry two Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and the family stays about ten years. Then both sons die too. In five short verses the storyteller moves from a family of four leaving home to three widows: Naomi and her two daughters-in-law. No cause is given for any of the deaths, and God is not named once. The rest of the book will follow these women, but it begins here, with loss told plainly.

Verse 5 says that Mahlon and Chilion both died, and Naomi was left without her two sons and without her husband. The verse does not hurry past this or try to explain it. It counts what is gone and lets the emptiness stand. Whatever happens later in Ruth, the book never asks us to pretend this did not happen. Hope, if it comes, will have to come into exactly this place.

**Illustration**

Think of a family photograph taken as they set out from Bethlehem: a man, a woman, two grown sons, bags at their feet. Later someone takes a second picture in Moab, and now two young wives stand in it as well, six people crowded into the frame. Then imagine the pictures that follow, each one with someone missing, until the last shows three women standing close together, with gaps around them where the men used to stand. Nobody in that last picture is posing. Verse 5 is something like that final photograph. It does not tell us what Naomi felt or what she said. It simply shows who is left, and lets us notice the spaces.

**Ponder**

- Why might the storyteller begin this book with such stark, unexplained loss and no mention of God?
- Where do you find it hard to name grief plainly before God without rushing to explain it?
- What do you notice about Orpah and Ruth, also widowed, though the verse speaks of Naomi?

**Prayer**

- Bring before God people who, like Naomi, have lost the ones they leaned on.
- Ask for honesty to name loss in God's presence without hurrying past it.
- Pray for families who have left home because of hunger or hardship, as this family left Bethlehem.

## Philippians 1:1-2
*Philippians · section 1 of 12 · key verse Philippians 1:1*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Paul and Timothy, servants of Jesus Christ; To all the saints in Christ Jesus who are at Philippi, with the overseers and servants: Grace to you, and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

</details>

**Thought**

The letter opens the way letters in its world usually did: who is writing, who is receiving, and a word of greeting. Christ shapes each part. Paul names Timothy beside him and calls them both servants of Christ, a word that can also mean slaves, and he claims no higher title. He writes to all the saints in Christ Jesus at Philippi, and he mentions the overseers and deacons as being with them, not above them. Then comes the greeting. Paul wishes them grace and peace, and he names God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together as where they come from.

Slow down on the first verse. Two words set the tone. Paul and Timothy are servants. The believers are saints, people set apart as belonging to God. Neither word rests on achievement. Servants belong to their master, and the saints are holy because they are in Christ Jesus. Notice that being in Christ Jesus comes before being at Philippi. Their city is real, but it is not the deepest place they live. Later in the letter Paul urges them to look to the interests of others. Even here, before he gives any instruction, he has put everyone on the same ground, leaders included.

**Illustration**

Think of an envelope. On the front is a name, a street and a town, everything a postal worker needs to find the right door. Now imagine the letter inside is from a parent, and it opens with a few words that say who this person is to the one writing: loved, known, belonging. The address on the envelope is accurate, but it tells you very little about who the person is. The opening of Paul's letter carries both kinds of address at once. The believers really do live at Philippi, among its streets and neighbours and pressures. Before he mentions the town, though, Paul has already said who they are and to whom they belong. They are saints, and they are in Christ Jesus. The town tells you where to find them. Being in Christ tells you who they are.

**Ponder**

- What changes when you think of yourself first as being in Christ, and only then by where you live?
- Why might Paul introduce himself simply as a servant, sharing that title equally with Timothy?
- What would it mean to receive grace and peace as gifts rather than things to earn?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that belonging to him rests on being in Christ, not on standing or achievement.
- Pray for those who oversee and serve in your church, and for the whole community alongside them.
- Ask God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ for grace and peace for someone you know.

**Checks:** machine pass

The verse only says the letter goes to the saints together with the overseers and deacons. Saying the leaders are not above the others is an interpretation presented as what the verse plainly says, and it takes a side on how church leadership should be structured, where Christians disagree.

## Leviticus 1:1-17
*Leviticus · section 1 of 81 · key verse Leviticus 1:4*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Yahweh called to Moses, and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting, saying, “Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When anyone of you offers an offering to Yahweh, you shall offer your offering of the livestock, from the herd and from the flock. “ ‘If his offering is a burnt offering from the herd, he shall offer a male without defect. He shall offer it at the door of the Tent of Meeting, that he may be accepted before Yahweh. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall present the blood and sprinkle the blood around on the altar that is at the door of the Tent of Meeting. He shall skin the burnt offering, and cut it into pieces. The priest shall burn all of it on the altar, for a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh. “ ‘If his offering is from the flock, from the sheep or from the goats, for a burnt offering, he shall offer a male without defect. He shall kill it on the north side of the altar before Yahweh. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall sprinkle its blood around on the altar. He shall cut it into its pieces, with its head and its fat. The priest shall lay them in order on the wood that is on the fire which is on the altar, but the innards and the legs he shall wash with water. The priest shall offer the whole, and burn it on the altar. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh. “ ‘If his offering to Yahweh is a burnt offering of birds, then he shall offer his offering from turtledoves or of young pigeons. The priest shall bring it to the altar, and wring off its head, and burn it on the altar; and its blood shall be drained out on the side of the altar; and he shall take away its crop and its feathers, and cast it beside the altar on the east part, in the place of the ashes. He shall tear it by its wings, but shall not divide it apart. The priest shall burn it on the altar, on the wood that is on the fire. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to Yahweh.

</details>

**Thought**

Leviticus opens with the LORD calling to Moses from the Tent of Meeting. His first words are about how an Israelite may come near with an offering. A burnt offering may be a bull, a sheep or goat, or a turtledove or young pigeon. When the offering is an animal from the herd or the flock, the worshipper kills, skins and cuts it, then washes its inner parts and legs. Aaron's sons the priests handle the blood, tend the fire and arrange the pieces on the altar. With birds, the priest does all of it. Each kind of offering ends the same way: the priest burns it on the altar as a pleasing aroma to the LORD. A dove ends just as a bull does.

In verse 4, the one bringing the animal lays his hand on its head, and it is accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him. He doesn't keep his distance from his gift. He rests his own hand on it, and God accepts it for him. Many Christians have seen here one life standing in for another, and have read it alongside the death of Jesus. At the very least, it shows this holy God setting out a way to draw near.

**Illustration**

Imagine a farmer standing at the entrance of the tent with a young bull from his own herd. He has fed it, watched it grow, and picked it because it had no flaw. Now he reaches out and presses his palm onto its broad head. He can feel how warm it is and the breath moving through it. Nothing about the moment is abstract. This isn't a payment sent from far away, or a form someone else signs for him. His own hand says that this animal is his, and that it comes before God for him. Think of how differently we hold something we are handing over when it costs us, and when it stands in our place. The hand on the head joins the giver to the gift, and the text says God accepts it for him.

**Ponder**

- What does it suggest about God that a pair of birds gets the same closing words as a bull?
- Why might the worshipper's own hand need to rest on the offering, rather than leaving it all to the priests?
- What does it stir in you that this book begins with God explaining how to come near him?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that he speaks first and makes a way for people to come near him.
- Offer God what you have to give, whether it seems large or small.
- Give thanks for atonement, and for Jesus, whom many Christians have seen pictured in this offering.

- The thought says that for an animal "from the herd or the flock, the worshipper kills, skins and cuts it". The passage mentions skinning only for the bull from the herd. For the sheep or goat from the flock, it says only that the worshipper kills it and cuts it into pieces.

**Thought**

An old man from Ephraim, living among Benjamites in Gibeah, takes the Levite and his party in from the square. Then men of the town surround the house and demand the guest. The host refuses, but he offers his own daughter and the concubine instead. When the men will not listen, the Levite puts his concubine out to them, and they rape and abuse her all night. At dawn she reaches the door and falls there. In the morning her master opens the door to leave and tells her to get up. No answer comes. He carries her home, cuts her body into twelve pieces and sends them through Israel. Those who see it say nothing like this has happened since Israel came out of Egypt.

Stay with verses 26 and 27. She comes back to the door of the house where her master was, and she lies there until it is light. The narrator notices her hands on the threshold, reaching toward the house whose men had handed her over. Her master's only words to her are about leaving. The text gives her no name and no voice, yet it will not let the reader step past her. Judges has been tracing Israel's collapse, and here it comes down to one doorway and one woman whom no one protected.

**Illustration**

Think of how a family often remembers a loss. It is not always the big facts that stay with them. Often it is one small detail that will not go away, like a coat still on its hook or a cup left on the table. That detail stops the person from turning into a case or a headline. The narrator does something like this with her hands on the threshold. Soon the Levite will turn her into a message to the tribes, a body cut into pieces and carried to every border. Before that happens, the story makes the reader look at her hands. Whatever the men of Gibeah did, and whatever her master did next, the narrator keeps one image of her as a person who reached for a door. The text does not look away here, and it does not invite its readers to look away either.

**Ponder**

- Why might the narrator record her hands on the threshold, when it tells us so little else about her?
- Where do you notice yourself wanting to look away from this story, and what might it mean to stay with it?
- What does it show about a people when even the host who refuses the mob offers up the women instead?

**Prayer**

- Grieve before God for this woman, unnamed and unprotected, and for all who suffer violence like hers.
- Ask God for eyes that see the vulnerable person whom others step past or hand over.
- Pray for those who hold power in homes and communities, that they would protect the weak rather than give them up.

## 1 Chronicles 1:1-4
*1 Chronicles · section 1 of 103 · key verse 1 Chronicles 1:1*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth.

</details>

**Thought**

Chronicles opens without a sentence. There is no setting, no speaker and no action, only names. It starts with Adam, and a single line of names runs from him to Noah. With Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham and Japheth, that one line divides into three. The chronicler tells none of their stories. He seems to trust that his readers know them from Genesis, so a whole life fits into one word. This book will go on to tell of David, his kingdom and his preparations for the temple in Jerusalem. Yet before Israel is mentioned at all, it begins with the first human being.

Slow down on the first verse: Adam, then Seth, then Enosh. Three names with nothing added. Even so, the choice of a starting point says something. The history of God's people sits inside the history of humanity. It is not a private story walled off from everyone else. It belongs to one family that begins with Adam. The list offers no praise and no blame. Being named is enough. Each person is kept in the record and remembered as part of a line that leads somewhere.

**Illustration**

Imagine an old family Bible with names written in faded ink inside the front cover. Most of them carry no stories. Nobody now remembers what that great-great-grandmother laughed at, or what the man two lines above her did for work. Still, someone took care to write each name, and the list starts as far back as anyone could reach before it comes down to the present household. To the family that keeps it, those bare names say: you did not come from nowhere, and you are not alone. The opening of Chronicles does something like this, but on a far larger scale. It reaches back past any one household, tribe or nation to Adam. The family record of Israel's kings and temple begins at the very source of the human family.

**Ponder**

- Why might a book about David and the temple begin with Adam instead of with Israel?
- What changes when you see God's people as part of the whole human family, not set apart from it?
- Whose names, with stories you will never know, stand somewhere behind your own life?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that the record of his people begins with the whole human family.
- Pray for peoples beyond your own, whom this list holds together in one family.
- Ask God for trust that he knows and remembers each person by name, even those whose stories go untold.

**Checks:** machine pass

reviewer 
- The second ponder question asks the reader to see God's people as part of the whole human family, "not set apart from it". That denies something Scripture plainly says: God's people are set apart, or holy. It also cuts against Chronicles itself, whose genealogies go on to narrow from all humanity down to Israel's chosen line. The passage places Israel within the human family; it does not say Israel is not set apart.

## Song of Solomon 1:1-4
*Song of Solomon · section 1 of 14 · key verse Song of Solomon 1:2-3*

<details><summary>Passage (WEB)</summary>

The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your love is better than wine. Your oils have a pleasing fragrance. Your name is oil poured out, therefore the virgins love you. Take me away with you. Let’s hurry. The king has brought me into his rooms. We will be glad and rejoice in you. We will praise your love more than wine! They are right to love you.

</details>

**Thought**

A title names this the Song of Songs, a Hebrew way of saying the finest song, and links it with Solomon. Then, without introduction, a woman speaks, and her first words are desire: she wants his kiss. She moves between speaking about him and speaking to him, as lovers do. His love delights her more than wine, and his fragrance and his name are lovely. She urges him to take her away quickly, and she speaks of the king's chambers. Some translations give this as something already done and others as something hoped for. Then other voices, young women around her, gladly join in and agree that loving him is right. The Song opens with longing spoken openly and welcomed.

Stay with verses 2 and 3. She says his love is better than wine and his name is like perfume poured out. A name stands for the whole person, and poured perfume fills a room. Who he is reaches beyond him and draws others in. Read first as love poetry, this honours desire between a woman and a man as good. Many Christians have also heard here a picture of God's love, and the poem leaves room for both readings.

**Illustration**

Think of what happens when someone uncorks a bottle of fragrant oil in a closed room. Within moments everyone notices. Nobody has to be told it is there. The scent moves through the air and reaches each person. Once poured out, it cannot also be kept private. Or think of how the name of someone dearly loved can sound different from every other word. Hear it spoken across a crowded room and something in you turns toward it, because the name carries the whole person with it. The woman in the Song puts these two pictures together. Her beloved's name is like that poured oil: it fills the space and reaches others, and the young women around her are drawn to him too. Her love does not resent their admiration. It agrees with them that he is worth loving.

**Ponder**

- What might it mean that this book opens with a woman voicing her own desire so openly?
- When you hear the name of God, what does it carry for you?
- Why might the gladness of her companions matter to the woman's love?

**Prayer**

- Give thanks for love between a woman and a man as a good gift from God.
- Pray for couples you know, that their love would be tender, honest and glad.
- Speak to God honestly about your own longings, trusting that he hears them without contempt.

- The reading treats one interpretation of verse 4 as plain fact: "Then other voices, young women around her, gladly join in and agree that loving him is right." The section never says who speaks the "we" lines. Many take them as the woman herself or as the woman with others. In the BSB, "they adore you" sets "they" apart from "we", which makes the speakers even less certain. This should be offered as one reading. The illustration builds on the same assumption with "Her love does not resent their admiration", which the passage does not say.
