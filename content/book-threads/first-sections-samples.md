## Genesis — section 1
*Key verse Genesis 1:27:* “God created man in his own image. In God’s image he created him; male and female he created them.”

**Thought**

The Bible opens with God already there. The earth is formless and empty, darkness lies over the deep, and God's Spirit hovers over the waters. Then God speaks, and things come to be. First he gives the world shape: he separates light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, and dry land from the seas. Then he fills it with plants bearing seed, with lights in the sky, with creatures swarming in the seas and birds in the sky, and with animals across the land. Again and again God looks and sees that it is good. On the sixth day he makes humankind, blesses them, gives them food and places the creatures under their rule. He looks at everything and finds it very good. On the seventh day he rests, and he blesses that day and makes it holy.

Look at verse 27. God creates humankind in his own image. The verse turns this over more than once, and its last line adds that he made them male and female. It lingers on whose likeness people carry, and gives that likeness to man and woman together.

**Illustration**

Imagine a small mirror propped on a windowsill. It has no light of its own. Lying face down, it is just a piece of glass. Turn it toward the sun, though, and it throws a patch of brightness into a dim corner of the room. What makes it worth looking at is the light it catches and passes on, not the glass.

Think too of a child whose face carries something of a parent's: the same eyes, the same way of smiling. The child didn't earn that resemblance or choose it; it came with birth. Both pictures fall short, as pictures of God do. Still, they help with verse 27, which does not spell out what the image is; Christians have understood it in several ways. Many have seen in it a calling to reflect God in the world he called good. The verse itself says God gave this likeness to humankind, male and female.

**Ponder**

- How might you see a stranger differently, knowing they too bear God's image?
- Where in the created world have you noticed something that God, looking at it, might call good?
- What do you make of God blessing the seventh day and making it holy because he rested on it?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for one particular part of his creation, and praise him as the one who made it good.
- Ask God to help you see his image in the people around you, including those you find hard to love.
- Rest for a moment before God, who finished his work and then blessed the seventh day and made it holy.

*Fixed in 2 round(s). Problems the reviewer sent back:*
- The third ponder question says God was "blessing rest and making it holy". The passage says God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because he rested on it. It does not say he blessed rest itself.
- The third prayer prompt speaks of "the stillness he blessed". The passage says God blessed the seventh day, not stillness, so this changes what the text says God blessed.
- The illustration says the image is "one meant to reflect him in the world he called good" and frames this as "what verse 27 says". The verse does not say what the image is for, and Christians have understood the image of God in several ways. This should be offered as one reading, not as the plain meaning of the text.
- The illustration ends with the absolute claim "it belongs to every man and every woman". Verse 27 says God created humankind in his image, "male and female". It never says "every", which breaks the guideline to use absolute words only when the passage does. The ponder question's "just as fully as you do" goes beyond the text in the same way.

## Leviticus — section 1
*Key verse Leviticus 1:4:* “He shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him.”

**Thought**

Leviticus opens with the LORD calling to Moses from the Tent of Meeting, and the first thing he speaks of is how an Israelite may bring an offering. The section describes the burnt offering in three forms: a male from the herd, a male sheep or goat from the flock, or a turtledove or young pigeon. Each goes up in smoke on the altar, and each paragraph ends the same way, as a pleasing aroma to the LORD. With a bull, the worshipper brings it to the entrance, kills it, skins it and cuts it up, while Aaron's sons sprinkle the blood, tend the fire and arrange the pieces. Many readers have noticed that the range of animals lets people of differing means come near.

Verse 4 carries the weight. The one bringing the offering lays his hand on the animal's head, and the offering is accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him. Many have understood that hand to say: this one stands for me. Many Christians have also seen a picture of Christ here. Notice who sets the terms: God himself opens the way for a person to be received.

**Illustration**

Think of a farmer leading his best young bull, without defect, away from the herd. He has raised it and fed it, and perhaps he was counting on it. At the entrance of the tent he stops and presses his hand down on its head. The animal can feel the weight of that hand, warm and deliberate. The gesture is neither hurried nor anonymous. It is not a priest's hand or a stranger's. It is his own. With that touch he is not just handing over property. He is joining himself to what is about to happen, so that it goes up before God connected with him. A signature works something like this: a little ink on paper, yet it binds a person to everything the page says. The hand on the head is a signature made by touch, and God accepts the offering for the one who signed.

**Ponder**

- Why might it matter that the worshipper lays his own hand on the animal, rather than leaving everything to the priests?
- What might it suggest about God that the paragraph on the pigeon ends with the same pleasing aroma as the paragraph on the bull?
- Where do you find it hardest to believe that you could be accepted before God?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that he himself gives a way for people to come near him and be accepted.
- Bring before God whatever you would rather hold back, and ask him to receive the whole of you.
- Pray for anyone who feels they have too little to offer, that they would know God welcomes them.

## Judges — section 1
*Key verse Judges 1:19:* “Yahweh was with Judah, and drove out the inhabitants of the hill country; for he could not drive out the inhabitants of the valley, because they had chariots of iron.”

**Thought**

Joshua is dead. Israel asks the LORD who should go first against the Canaanites, and he names Judah, saying he has given the land into Judah's hand. With Simeon, Judah defeats the Canaanites and Perizzites at Bezek. They catch the fleeing king Adoni-bezek and cut off his thumbs and big toes. He calls it God repaying him for what he did to seventy kings. Judah takes Jerusalem and burns it. Othniel captures Debir, and Caleb gives him his daughter Achsah, who asks her father for springs and receives the upper and lower springs. The Kenites settle among the people. Judah and Simeon strike the Canaanites of Zephath and destroy the city completely, and it is renamed Hormah. Judah captures Gaza, Ashkelon and Ekron, and gives Hebron to Caleb, as Moses had said.

Then verse 19 sets two things side by side. The LORD was with Judah, and Judah won the hill country. Yet Judah could not drive out the people of the valley, who had iron chariots. The text does not smooth this over: the promise stands, and so does the limit.

**Illustration**

Imagine someone hands you the deed to a farm. The papers are signed, and in law the land is yours. You walk the upper fields freely. But when you reach the lower valley, you find other people still farming it, with fences and guards, and you cannot get in. Nothing about the deed has changed, and nothing about the valley has changed either. You stand at the fence holding both facts at once. Verse 19 leaves Judah at a fence like that. The writer does not pretend the valley was taken. Nor does he suggest the LORD had stopped being with Judah. Readers have long wondered whether iron chariots were really too much for the God who had promised the land, or whether something in Judah faltered. The verse does not answer, and Judges presses the question further.

**Ponder**

- Why might the writer say in one breath that the LORD was with Judah and that Judah could not drive out the valley's people?
- Where do God's promises and your own limits seem to stand side by side, without explanation?
- What might Achsah's bold request for springs suggest about asking freely of one who has already given?

**Prayer**

- Bring to God the places where his promise feels certain but your hold on it feels partial.
- Like Achsah, ask plainly for what you need to live and flourish where God has set you.
- Pray for people whose lives are scarred by war and by the cruelty of powerful rulers.

*Fixed in 2 round(s). Problems the reviewer sent back:*
- quote "the LORD was with Judah" is not word-for-word in WEB
- time phrase you have been
- time phrase keep coming back
- The summary line "Zephath becomes Hormah, Gaza, Ashkelon and Ekron fall" leaves out who acted and what they did. The passage says Judah and Simeon struck the Canaanites of Zephath and utterly destroyed (devoted to destruction) the city, which is why it was renamed Hormah, and that Judah captured the three Philistine cities. The wording softens the violence and hides the agents, against the guidelines' rules to say who does what and to name violence plainly.

## 1 Chronicles — section 1
*Key verse 1 Chronicles 1:1:* “Adam, Seth, Enosh,”

**Thought**

1 Chronicles opens without a sentence. Nobody speaks, nothing happens and there is no setting. There are just thirteen names, running from Adam to Noah and then to Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham and Japheth. Readers who know Genesis will hear whole stories packed into single words like Enoch, Methuselah and Noah. Here the writer simply lays the names down in order: ten generations, then a branching into three, as if everything that follows rests on them. The book will go on to tell of David, of worship and of the temple. Even so, it does not begin with Israel. It begins with humanity.

Stay with the first verse. It names Adam, then Seth, then Enosh, and nothing more. Before any tribe, king or city, there is the first man as the text presents him, with his son and his grandson. Whatever God does with one people later in the book, the writer roots it in a story that belongs to every people. Notice too that these names are remembered at all. None of them is given a deed here. They are kept because they belonged to the line, and someone thought them worth writing down.

**Illustration**

Think of the front pages of an old family Bible, where people have written names over generations in different hands: births, marriages, deaths. A great-grandchild turning those pages may know stories about a few of them. Most are just names and dates. Even so, no one would tear out the page because the names are unfamiliar. The list tells the child something no single story could: you did not begin with yourself. Others came before you, and someone cared enough to keep their names. The opening of Chronicles works something like that, except that this page starts with Adam. Whoever opens it, wherever they come from, finds their own beginning written at the top.

**Ponder**

- What might it mean that a book about Israel's worship and kings begins with Adam rather than with Abraham or David?
- How do you feel about the fact that many people of faith are remembered by little more than a name?
- Where do you see your own life inside a story much older than yourself?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that the story of his people is rooted in the story of all humanity.
- Remember before God the people who came before you, the named and the forgotten alike.
- Ask for trust that your life is held within a larger story than you can see.

## Song of Solomon — section 1
*Key verse Song of Solomon 1:2-3:* “Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your love is better than wine. Your oils have a pleasing fragrance. Your name is oil poured out, therefore the virgins love you.”

**Thought**

The book opens with a title that calls this the song of songs, meaning the finest of songs, and connects it with Solomon. Then a woman speaks, with no introduction. She longs for her beloved's kiss. Like many people in love, she moves back and forth between speaking about him and speaking to him. His love delights her more than wine, his fragrance is pleasing, and even his name spreads like perfume poured out, so it is no wonder the young women love him. She asks him to take her away quickly, and she speaks of the king's rooms. Then other voices join hers, rejoicing in him and agreeing that loving him is right. Read first for what it is, this is love poetry, and it begins with a woman's desire spoken openly. Many Christians have also heard in it a picture of God's love for his people. Verses 2 and 3 set the tone. She prizes his love above wine, itself a sign of feasting and gladness, and even the mention of his name fills the air like scent. Her delight is in him.

**Illustration**

Think of opening a bottle of perfume in a closed room. Before long the scent reaches every corner, and people who never saw the bottle know it is there. You cannot pour it out and keep it to yourself. That is the picture the woman reaches for when she speaks of his name. In Scripture a name often stands for the whole person, their character and their reputation. His name goes ahead of him and fills the room, and others notice it too, which is why the young women love him. She does not resent that. She is glad of it, and she agrees with them. Something similar happens when we speak of someone we love. Their name alone can change the air in a room, bringing warmth, memory and longing before they have even walked in.

**Ponder**

- What do you make of a book of Scripture that opens with a woman's desire spoken so openly?
- Whose name, when you hear it, carries something like the fragrance the woman describes?
- Why might this song begin with longing rather than with a story of how the two met?

**Prayer**

- Thank God for the gift of love and desire between a woman and a man as part of his good creation.
- Thank God that Scripture makes room for longing and delight to be spoken honestly.
- Pray for those for whom love has brought pain rather than delight.

*Fixed in 1 round(s). Problems the reviewer sent back:*
- The thought says "She wants the man himself, not only what he brings," but verses 2–3 never mention anything he brings or gives, so the contrast is added to the passage. The prayer "delight in him for who he is, not only for what he gives" is built on that added idea.
- The ponder question "Where does your love for God feel more like duty than delight?" assumes the reader's love for God feels like duty somewhere, which is a claim about the reader's inner life that the writer cannot know.
- The duty-versus-delight question and the prayer to delight in God "for who he is" treat the woman's longing for her beloved as a model for loving God. That quietly takes the allegorical reading as the plain meaning, even though the thought itself presents it only as one way many Christians have read the book.

## Lamentations — section 1
*Key verse Lamentations 1:12:* ““Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look, and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow, which is brought on me, with which Yahweh has afflicted me in the day of his fierce anger.”

**Thought**

Lamentations opens over a city gone quiet. Jerusalem, once crowded and honored among the nations, sits like a widow weeping through the night. A narrator describes her: empty roads to Zion, groaning priests, children taken captive, enemies at ease, people trading their treasures for bread, nations entering the sanctuary. The poem says plainly that the LORD has afflicted her for her many transgressions. Twice Zion breaks in, begging the LORD to look. Then she takes up the voice herself: fire in her bones, a yoke of sin on her neck, her people trodden as in a winepress. One line keeps coming back: no one comforts her. She confesses that the LORD is righteous. She ends by asking him to deal with her enemies as he has dealt with her, and she says her heart is faint.

In verse 12 Zion turns to those passing by and asks whether her suffering means nothing to them. She asks them to look and see whether any sorrow is like hers, a sorrow she names as the LORD's doing. She does not ask for an explanation. She asks to be seen, and Scripture keeps her cry.

**Illustration**

Imagine someone standing outside a house that has just burned down, a blanket around her shoulders, while traffic keeps moving along the road. A few drivers slow down to look. Most do not. Nobody who stopped could rebuild the walls or undo what happened. Still, someone who pulls over, gets out and simply stands beside her for a while does something real. The fire is still the fire, but she is no longer alone with it. Zion's question to the passers-by works something like this. Again and again the narrator says that no one comforts her, and then she says it herself. What she pleads for first is not repair. She wants someone to stop and look. Notice that she also asks the LORD, again and again, to look. The poem keeps asking whether God, too, will stop and see.

**Ponder**

- When have you hurried past someone's sorrow because you did not know what to say?
- What do you make of Zion confessing her sin and pouring out her grief in the same breath?
- Is there a sorrow you hesitate to ask God to look at?

**Prayer**

- Ask God to look on people whose cities and homes lie in ruins.
- Pray for the courage to stop beside someone else's grief instead of passing by.
- Bring to God, honestly, any sorrow that feels unseen.

*Fixed in 1 round(s). Problems the reviewer sent back:*
- The illustration says "All through the poem she says that no one comforts her," but in most places it is the narrator who says Jerusalem has no comforter (verses 2, 9, 17); Zion says it in her own voice only in verses 16 and 21, so the line credits her with the narrator's words.

## Jonah — section 1
*Key verse Jonah 1:3:* “But Jonah rose up to flee to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh. He went down to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid its fare, and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh.”

**Thought**

The word of the LORD comes to Jonah son of Amittai. The command is short: get up, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry out against it, because its wickedness has come up before God. The next sentence begins as if Jonah will obey. He gets up. But he gets up to run the other way, toward Tarshish, a port far off across the sea. Twice the narrator says he is going away from the presence of the LORD. The section gives no reason for his flight. The rest of the book grows out of this refusal.

Slow down on verse 3. Every step is ordinary. Jonah walks to the harbour at Joppa, finds a ship going where he wants, pays the fare and climbs aboard. The text mentions no obstacle. Yet the narrator keeps naming what is really happening: a prophet is trying to distance himself from the God who sees what goes on in a distant city. Notice the direction. Jonah goes down to Joppa, and in the original language he goes down again into the ship. Running from God is pictured as a descent.

**Illustration**

Think of a traveller standing under a departure board. A seat is free on the next flight, and the card goes through on the first try. Security is quick, the plane leaves on time, and the clouds close in below the window. Everything works. It is easy to take that kind of smoothness as a sign that the decision was right, as if a door wouldn't open unless it was meant to.

Jonah's escape looks like that. A ship is waiting at Joppa, it is headed for Tarshish, and he can pay for a place on board. The narrator mentions no delay or difficulty. The narrator, though, doesn't judge the plan by how easily it comes together. He judges it by where it is heading, which is away from the presence of the LORD. A route can be wide open and still lead away from God.

**Ponder**

- Why might the narrator mention twice in one verse that Jonah was going away from the LORD's presence?
- What might it suggest that the narrator tells of Jonah finding a ship, paying and boarding, and mentions no hitch?
- Where might an easy, open path be mistaken for God's approval in your own life?

**Prayer**

- Thank God that his word comes to people, including those who turn away from it.
- Ask God for honesty about any place where you are quietly heading the other way from him.
- Pray for great cities whose wrongs God sees, that his word would reach them.

*Fixed in 1 round(s). Problems the reviewer sent back:*
- The reading uses absolutes the passage never states: "Nothing gets in his way" (thought), "Not one detail goes wrong" (illustration) and "with nothing standing in his way" (ponder). The section only says that Jonah found a ship, paid the fare and boarded. It does not claim his way was free of every obstacle, and the guidelines allow absolute words only when the passage itself uses them.

## Revelation — section 1
*Key verse Revelation 1:5-6:* “and from Jesus Christ, the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead, and the ruler of the kings of the earth. To him who loves us, and washed us from our sins by his blood— and he made us to be a Kingdom, priests to his God and Father—to him be the glory and the dominion forever and ever. Amen.”

**Thought**

Revelation opens by naming whose it is: an unveiling of Jesus Christ, given by God and made known through an angel to John, who bears witness to what he saw. A blessing rests on the one who reads and on those who hear and keep it, for the time is near. John greets seven churches in Asia with grace and peace from the One who is, was and is to come, and from the seven Spirits before the throne, which many Christians read as the Holy Spirit in his fullness. The greeting also comes from Jesus. It swells into praise, then into a cry that he is coming with the clouds. The Lord God himself has the last word: Alpha and Omega, the Almighty.

Slow down at verses 5 and 6. Jesus is the faithful witness, the firstborn from the dead and the ruler of earth's kings. Then John praises what he does. He loves his people, he has freed them from their sins by his blood, and he has made them a kingdom and priests to his God and Father. Before a single vision unfolds, the book sets down this love.

**Illustration**

Think of an orchestra before a concert. Before the symphony begins, one instrument sounds a single note, and every other player tunes to it. Whatever comes after, however loud or strange or stormy the music becomes, it is played in relation to that first note. The opening of Revelation works something like that. Much of what follows in the book is dramatic and hard to understand, and readers can easily lose their footing in it. But before any of it, John sounds the note: Jesus loves his people, has freed them from their sins at the cost of his own blood, and has made them a kingdom and priests. Imagine a listener who keeps that note in mind through the whole performance. The strange passages stay strange, but they no longer sound like noise. They are heard against the love of the One who rules the kings of the earth.

**Ponder**

- Why might it matter that Revelation praises Jesus' love before it shows any of its visions?
- What would it look like to treat this book as something to keep, not just something to figure out?
- How does Jesus as ruler of earth's kings fit with Jesus as the one who loves you?

**Prayer**

- Thank Jesus for his love and for freeing his people from their sins by his blood.
- Ask for ears to hear this book and a heart that keeps what is written in it.
- Worship the Lord God, who is and was and is to come, the Almighty.
