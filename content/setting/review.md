## Books

### Genesis

**Who wrote it.** Genesis does not name its author. Jewish and Christian tradition holds that it was written by Moses, an Israelite raised in the Egyptian royal court who later led his people out of slavery and served as their prophet and lawgiver. Many scholars instead think the book was woven together over centuries from several older collections of stories and family records. On that view it reached its final form around the time of Israel's exile in Babylon or shortly after, in the 500s BC.

**Who first heard it.** On the traditional view, the first hearers were the Israelites who had escaped Egypt and were living as wandering tribes in the desert, on their way to settle in Canaan, the land later called Israel. Scholars who favour a later date picture Israelites in or just after exile in Babylon, a people who had lost their land, king and temple. In either setting, these were people asking who they were and where they had come from.

**What was happening.** The stories themselves are set long before they were written down: the families of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob are usually placed somewhere around 2000–1500 BC, moving between Mesopotamia (modern Iraq, Syria and south-east Turkey), Canaan and Egypt. That world was made up of great river kingdoms, small city-states and herding families who travelled with their flocks in search of pasture and water. The surrounding peoples, especially in Babylon, told their own stories of creation and of a great flood. The book was written down either around the 1400s–1200s BC, on the traditional view, or as late as the 500s BC on the main alternative.

### Exodus

**Who wrote it.** Jewish and Christian tradition holds that Moses wrote Exodus. He was an Israelite raised in the household of Egypt's royal family, then spent years as a shepherd in the desert before becoming the leader, lawgiver and prophet who brought his people out of Egypt. Many modern scholars think the book was woven together later from several older written and spoken sources, perhaps reaching its final form around the time of the Babylonian exile (500s BC), while still drawing on very old traditions.

**Who first heard it.** On the traditional view, Exodus was first meant for the Israelites who had escaped Egypt and were living as a wandering people in the desert, learning to live as a nation. On the later-compilation view, it was gathered for Israelites centuries afterward, many of them exiled or newly returned home, who needed to remember where they came from.

**What was happening.** Egypt was the great power of the day, ruled by pharaohs who used forced labour from foreign peoples to build cities and monuments. The date of the escape from Egypt is debated: some place it around 1446 BC, others around 1250 BC in the reign of Ramesses II. After leaving Egypt the Israelites lived as nomads in the harsh Sinai desert, between Egypt and the land of Canaan. Neighbouring peoples worshipped many gods and had their own law codes and sacred shrines.

### Leviticus

**Who wrote it.** Jewish and Christian tradition holds that Moses wrote Leviticus. He was an Israelite raised in the Egyptian royal court, later a shepherd in the desert, and then the leader who brought his people out of slavery and passed on God's laws to them. Many modern scholars think the book took its final shape centuries later, gathered by priests from older traditions, perhaps during or soon after the Babylonian exile. Either way, it reads as a manual for worship, largely concerned with the work of priests from the tribe of Levi, which gives the book its name.

**Who first heard it.** The book presents itself as instructions given to the Israelites camped at Mount Sinai, newly freed from Egypt, and especially to Aaron and his sons, the first priests. They were a large, wandering people learning how to live and worship together before entering the land of Canaan. If the book was compiled later, its first readers would also have included Israelites around the exile who were trying to keep their worship and identity intact.

**What was happening.** In the traditional view the setting is the Late Bronze Age, roughly the 1400s or 1200s BC, when Egypt was the great power and Canaan was a patchwork of small city-states. Leviticus picks up just after the end of Exodus, when the Israelites had finished building the tabernacle, or "tent of meeting", a portable tent-shrine where they believed God's presence lived among them. The whole book covers only about a month at Sinai. Animal sacrifice and rules about ritual "cleanness", meaning fitness to take part in worship, were normal features of religion across the ancient world.

### Numbers

**Who wrote it.** Jewish and Christian tradition names Moses as the author of Numbers and the other four books of the Law. Moses was an Israelite raised in the Egyptian royal court who became the prophet and leader who brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt and through the desert. Many scholars instead see the book as gathered from older records, laws and stories over several centuries, reaching its final form around the time of the exile in Babylon or soon after (500s–400s BC).

**Who first heard it.** Traditionally, the first hearers were the new generation of Israelites camped on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan River, about to enter the land of Canaan after their parents' generation had died in the desert. Many scholars also see the finished book as speaking to later Israelites, possibly exiles in Babylon or those returning home, who were themselves waiting to settle again in the promised land.

**What was happening.** The book covers roughly forty years of Israel's life as a wandering people in the desert, from Mount Sinai to the edge of Canaan. The date of the exodus from Egypt is debated, with the two main views placing it in the 1400s BC or the 1200s BC. Canaan was then a patchwork of small walled city-states, often under Egyptian influence, surrounded by neighbouring peoples such as the Edomites, Moabites, Amorites and Midianites. Israel lived in tents, organised by tribes and clans, with the portable worship tent called the tabernacle at the centre of the camp.

### Deuteronomy

**Who wrote it.** Deuteronomy presents itself as the farewell speeches of Moses, the leader who brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt and received God's law at Mount Sinai. Raised in the Egyptian royal court, he spent forty years guiding the people through the wilderness as prophet, lawgiver and judge. Jewish and Christian tradition has long held Moses as its author, though the account of his death in the final chapter was added by someone else. Many scholars think the book drew on old traditions from Moses, but that its core was shaped centuries later by priests or scribes in Judah around the time of King Josiah (about 620 BC), with further additions during or after the exile in Babylon.

**Who first heard it.** The speeches are addressed to the Israelites camped on the plains of Moab, just east of the Jordan River, after forty years in the wilderness. Most were the children of those who had left Egypt, and they were about to cross into Canaan without Moses to lead them. If the book took its final shape later, it also spoke to the people of Judah in Josiah's day and to those later carried into exile in Babylon.

**What was happening.** On the traditional dating, this is somewhere between about 1400 and 1200 BC, when Canaan was a patchwork of small walled city-states whose local kings answered to Egypt. Israel had just passed the lands of Edom, Moab and Ammon and defeated two Amorite kings, Sihon and Og, east of the Jordan. The book is laid out much like ancient treaties between a great king and a smaller nation. Scholars who favour a later date point to the seventh century BC, when Assyria dominated the region and King Josiah carried out sweeping religious reforms around 622 BC.

### Joshua

**Who wrote it.** The book is named after Joshua son of Nun, from the tribe of Ephraim, who served as Moses' assistant and then led Israel's army after Moses died. Jewish tradition holds that Joshua wrote most of it himself, with the account of his death added afterward by the priests Eleazar and Phinehas. Many scholars instead think it was gathered and shaped centuries later, drawing on older records, lists and stories, as part of a longer history of Israel that runs from Deuteronomy through Kings.

**Who first heard it.** If Joshua wrote it, the first readers were the Israelites who had crossed the Jordan with him and were settling in Canaan, the land promised to their ancestors. If it was shaped later, it was read by Israelites under their kings, or by exiles in Babylon who had lost that same land and were looking back on how it was first received.

**What was happening.** The events take place in Canaan, roughly modern Israel and the West Bank, either around 1400 BC by the traditional reckoning or around 1250–1200 BC as many scholars suggest. Canaan was a patchwork of small walled cities, each ruled by its own king, loosely under the shadow of Egypt. Its people worshipped many gods, especially the storm god Baal. Archaeologists still debate how the evidence on the ground fits the book's account of these years.

### Judges

**Who wrote it.** Judges does not name its author. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud (an ancient collection of Jewish teaching), credits the prophet Samuel, the last of Israel's leaders before the kings. Many scholars instead think the book was gathered from older stories and songs and shaped by later Israelite writers, possibly during the time of the kings or even after the exile.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were Israelites looking back on their nation's early, chaotic days in the land. The book's repeated remark that there was no king in Israel suggests they already lived under kings and could compare those times with their own. If the book reached its final form later, it would also have spoken to people facing defeat and exile.

**What was happening.** The stories cover the generations between Joshua's conquest and the rise of King Saul. Most scholars place them around 1200 to 1050 BC, though a traditional reckoning based on numbers given in the Bible starts the period earlier, in the 1300s BC. Israel was a loose group of twelve tribes with no capital, no king and no shared army. Canaanite city-states still held strong positions, and neighbouring peoples such as the Moabites, Midianites and Ammonites raided Israelite land. Around this time the Philistines settled on the southern coast and became Israel's most dangerous rivals.

### Ruth

**Who wrote it.** The book of Ruth does not name its author. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud, credits the prophet Samuel, the last of Israel's judges, who anointed its first kings, Saul and David. Many scholars instead think an unnamed Israelite storyteller wrote it later, either during the time of the kings or after the Jewish exile in Babylon.

**Who first heard it.** The first hearers were Israelites, and the book ends by tracing the family line of David, Israel's great king, back to Ruth. If Samuel wrote it, as Jewish tradition holds, David's reign still lay ahead for those first hearers. If it was written during the time of the kings, they knew David as their great king and were living under rulers from his family. If it was written after the exile, they were a small community rebuilding life in Judah and wrestling with questions about marriage to foreigners.

**What was happening.** The story is set in the days of the judges, roughly 1200–1050 BC, before Israel had a king, when the tribes were loosely bound together and times were often unsettled. Life in a small town like Bethlehem revolved around farming, family land and the harvest seasons, and relatives were expected to look after kin who fell on hard times. Moab, Ruth's homeland, lay east of the Dead Sea and was a long-standing neighbour of Israel and sometimes its enemy. The book itself was written down later, perhaps near the end of this period, or anywhere up to several centuries afterwards, depending on who wrote it.

### 1 Samuel

**Who wrote it.** The book never names its author. Jewish tradition credits Samuel, the prophet, priest and judge raised at the sanctuary of Shiloh, with the prophets Nathan and Gad finishing it, since Samuel dies partway through. Many scholars think it was compiled later from older records, songs and stories, perhaps as part of a longer history running from Joshua to Kings, shaped around the time of the Babylonian exile in the 500s BC. First and Second Samuel were originally a single book.

**Who first heard it.** It was written for the people of Israel, telling how their nation came to have kings and how David's family came to the throne. If it reached its final form during the exile, its first readers were Judeans living in Babylon after Jerusalem fell in 586 BC, having lost their king, their temple and their land.

**What was happening.** The events took place roughly between 1100 and 1000 BC, as Israel moved from a loose group of tribes, led by occasional leaders called judges, to a single kingdom under Saul and then David. The Philistines, a seafaring people who had settled Canaan's southern coast in five strong cities, were pressing inland and, the book says, kept the Israelites from having blacksmiths of their own. The great powers of Egypt and Mesopotamia were weak at the time, leaving room for small nations to rise and fight over the land.

### 2 Samuel

**Who wrote it.** The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credited the prophets Samuel, Nathan and Gad, though Samuel dies before these events begin. Many scholars think it was put together later, perhaps around the time of the exile in Babylon (500s BC), by writers who wove older court records and stories into one long history of Israel. Others hold that much of it was written much earlier, close to David's own time. 1 and 2 Samuel were originally a single book.

**Who first heard it.** It was first written for the people of Israel, especially those who cared about King David and his royal family line. If the book reached its final form around the exile, its first readers had lost their land, temple and king, and were looking back on how their kingdom began.

**What was happening.** The events cover David's reign, around 1010 to 970 BC. Israel's twelve tribes were only loosely joined, and the Philistines, a powerful people on the coast, were a constant threat. David united the tribes, made Jerusalem his capital, and fought neighbouring peoples such as the Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites and Arameans. Kings in the region kept large households with several wives, and fights over who would inherit the throne were common.

### 1 Kings

**Who wrote it.** The writer is not named. Jewish tradition credits the prophet Jeremiah, while many scholars think an unnamed editor or circle of editors shaped the book during the exile in Babylon. Whoever it was drew on older royal records the book itself names, such as the annals of Solomon and the chronicles of the kings of Israel and Judah. First and Second Kings were originally one book, split later for convenience.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were most likely Jews living after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, many of them exiles in Babylon. Their kings, temple and homeland were gone, and they were asking how their nation had come to this.

**What was happening.** The book covers roughly 970 to 850 BC, from King David's death and Solomon's reign to the split of the kingdom and the rival kings of Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Small kingdoms such as Tyre, Aram and Moab traded and fought around them, while Egypt and later Assyria loomed as great powers. The finished work, ending in 2 Kings, dates from after about 560 BC, when Babylon had destroyed Jerusalem and its temple.

### 2 Kings

**Who wrote it.** The writer of 2 Kings is not named; 1 and 2 Kings were originally one book. Jewish tradition credits the prophet Jeremiah. Many scholars instead see it as the work of unnamed editors, possibly priests or scribes, who shaped a larger history running from Deuteronomy through Kings, perhaps with a first edition under King Josiah and a final one during the exile. Either way, the writer drew on older royal records and stories of the prophets, some of which are named in the book.

**Who first heard it.** The finished book was first read by Jewish people after the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC (some date it 587), many of them living as exiles in Babylon and others among the ruins at home. With no king, no temple and no land of their own, they were asking how their nation had come to fall.

**What was happening.** The book covers roughly 850 to 560 BC, when God's people were split into two kingdoms: Israel in the north, with its capital at Samaria, and Judah in the south, ruled from Jerusalem by kings descended from David. Great empires pressed in on them. Assyria conquered Israel in 722 BC, and Babylon later destroyed Jerusalem and its temple. The book's last event, around 561 BC, shows it was completed during the exile.

### 1 Chronicles

**Who wrote it.** Jewish tradition names Ezra, the priest and scribe who led a group of exiles home from Babylon and taught God's law in Jerusalem. Many scholars instead think the author was an unnamed writer, or a circle of writers, probably Levites (temple assistants and musicians) connected to the rebuilt temple, often simply called 'the Chronicler.' Either way, the writer worked from older records, including the books of Samuel and Kings, genealogies and temple lists, retelling Israel's story with special attention to David and to worship.

**Who first heard it.** It was written for Jews living in and around Jerusalem after the exile in Babylon, a small community rebuilding their lives under foreign rule. They had a temple again but no king of David's line, and many wondered whether they were still connected to the Israel of old and to God's promises.

**What was happening.** The book was most likely written in the Persian period, around 450–350 BC, though some scholars place it a little later, early in the Greek era. Judah was then a small province of the vast Persian Empire. The temple had been rebuilt around 516 BC, and leaders like Ezra and Nehemiah had worked to restore the community's worship, walls and identity. The events the book describes, chiefly David's reign, lay more than five hundred years in the past.

### 2 Chronicles

**Who wrote it.** Jewish tradition credits Ezra, a priest and scribe who helped lead the return from exile in Babylon. Many scholars instead think of an unnamed writer, often called 'the Chronicler', probably a Levite (a member of the tribe that served at the temple) living in Jerusalem. Either way, the writer worked from older records, including the books of Samuel and Kings, and clearly loved the temple, its music and its worship. First and Second Chronicles were originally one book.

**Who first heard it.** It was written for Jews who had come back from exile in Babylon to a small, poor homeland around Jerusalem, ruled by foreign powers. They had a rebuilt temple but no king, and they were asking whether God's promises to David and to their ancestors still held for them.

**What was happening.** Most scholars date the book to around 400–350 BC, when the Persian Empire ruled the Jewish province called Judah. Jerusalem had been destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BC, and the Persian king Cyrus later let exiles return and rebuild the temple. Life centred on the temple and its priests rather than a royal court. The book looks back over about four hundred years of Judah's kings, from Solomon to the exile.

### Ezra

**Who wrote it.** Jewish tradition holds that Ezra himself wrote this book. He was a priest descended from Aaron, Israel's first high priest, and a scribe, meaning an expert who copied, studied and taught the law of Moses. Several chapters are told in his own voice, and the book also includes official Persian letters and lists of families. Many scholars think a later editor, possibly the same person or circle behind the books of Chronicles, put the book together, perhaps around 400 BC, using Ezra's own memoirs and these documents. Ezra and Nehemiah were originally counted as a single book.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were Jews living in and around Jerusalem after the exile, when many of their families had been taken captive to Babylon. They were a small, often poor community inside the vast Persian Empire. They were rebuilding their temple, their city and their life as a people, and often faced suspicious neighbours and distant officials.

**What was happening.** In 539 BC the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon, and he soon allowed exiled peoples, including the Jews, to return home. Judah became a small district in a Persian province, governed under a king who lived far away. The book covers eighty years or more: the first return and the rebuilding of the temple, completed around 515 BC, and then, after a long gap (nearly sixty years on the traditional dating), Ezra's own journey to Jerusalem.

### Nehemiah

**Who wrote it.** Much of this book is the personal memoir of Nehemiah, a Jewish man living in exile who served as cupbearer to the Persian king Artaxerxes I. A cupbearer tasted and served the king's wine, a trusted post close to the throne. The king later appointed Nehemiah governor of Judah. Jewish tradition treats Ezra and Nehemiah as one book, possibly put together by Ezra, while many scholars think a later editor combined Nehemiah's memoir with other records and lists.

**Who first heard it.** It was first read by the small Jewish community in and around Jerusalem, descendants of exiles who had come back from Babylon. Traditionally the book is placed soon after Nehemiah's time, though many scholars think it reached its final form somewhat later, around 400 BC or after. Its first readers were poor, few in number and surrounded by unfriendly neighbours, and their city's walls had been rebuilt in Nehemiah's day.

**What was happening.** Judah was a small province of the vast Persian Empire. The Babylonians had destroyed Jerusalem around 586 BC, and from around 538 BC Persian kings allowed Jewish exiles to return. The temple was rebuilt by around 516 BC, but the city walls remained broken. Nehemiah's work begins around 445 BC, in the reign of Artaxerxes I.

### Esther

**Who wrote it.** The writer of Esther is not named. Jewish tradition credits the book to the 'Men of the Great Assembly,' a council of Jewish leaders after the exile, and some have suggested Mordecai himself. Many scholars think an unknown Jewish author wrote it some decades or more after the events, in the later Persian period or a little after. Whoever it was knew the Persian royal court well and wrote as a skilled storyteller.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were Jews scattered far from Jerusalem, in lands ruled by Persia or, if the book was written a little later, by the Greek kings who came after. They were a small minority living under foreign rulers, often exposed to suspicion and danger. The book also gave them the background to the yearly feast of Purim, which they kept together as a community.

**What was happening.** The story takes place in Susa, one of the Persian Empire's royal capitals, during the reign of Ahasuerus, usually identified with Xerxes I (around 486–465 BC). Persia then ruled a vast territory stretching from India to Cush, the land south of Egypt (some Bibles translate it 'Ethiopia'). Decades earlier King Cyrus had allowed exiled Jews to return home, but many had stayed where they were, building lives in Persian cities. Many readers take the story as straightforward history; some scholars see it as a tale set in a real Persian court rather than a record of events, partly because no record outside the Bible mentions Esther and Greek historians give Xerxes a queen with a different name.

### Job

**Who wrote it.** No one knows who wrote Job, and the book never names its author. Jewish tradition credited it to Moses, and some have suggested Solomon or Job himself, but many scholars think it was written by an unnamed Israelite poet steeped in Israel's 'wisdom' tradition, the kind of teachers who reflected on how life works. Whoever wrote it was a master of Hebrew poetry with a remarkably rich vocabulary, and framed the long poetic heart of the book with a short prose story at the beginning and end.

**Who first heard it.** Its first readers were Israelites who knew the wisdom teaching of their day, which taught that the good tend to prosper and the wicked to suffer. If the book comes from the time of exile or soon after, as many think, it spoke to a people who had lost their land, temple and king and were wrestling with why such suffering had come upon them.

**What was happening.** The story is set in an older world much like the days of Abraham. Wealth is counted in flocks, herds and servants, Job offers sacrifices himself as head of his family, and there is no mention of Israel, its law or its temple. Job lives in the land of Uz, probably in Edom or northern Arabia, east of Israel. When the book was actually written is uncertain. The Jewish tradition that Moses wrote it places it very early; other proposals range from Solomon's time, around 950 BC, to after the exile, around 500–400 BC, with many scholars favouring the later period.

### Psalms

**Who wrote it.** Psalms is a collection of 150 songs and prayers by many writers over several centuries. The headings credit about half to King David, a shepherd who became Israel's warrior-king and a skilled musician. Others are credited to Asaph and the 'sons of Korah' (families of Levites who led temple music), to King Solomon, and one to Moses, while many carry no name. Many scholars think the headings were added later, and they debate whether the headings always mean authorship, since 'of David' can also mean 'for' or 'in the style of' David.

**Who first heard it.** These songs were first sung by the people of Israel in worship, especially at the temple in Jerusalem, where Levite choirs led with harps, lyres, cymbals and trumpets. Over time the collection became Israel's hymnbook and prayer book, used by pilgrims at festivals, by Jews returning from exile, and later in synagogues.

**What was happening.** The psalms span many centuries. Tradition traces the oldest to Moses, though many scholars date the earliest to around King David's time, about 1000 BC, when Jerusalem became the capital and Solomon then built the temple. They cover the divided kingdoms, threats from Assyria and Babylon, Jerusalem's destruction in 586 BC and the exile in Babylon. The collection likely reached its final form after the return under Persian rule, when the temple was rebuilt around 516 BC.

### Proverbs

**Who wrote it.** Proverbs is traditionally linked to Solomon, son of David, king of Israel around 950 BC, remembered for his great wisdom and for speaking thousands of proverbs. The book itself names other contributors too: unnamed 'wise' teachers, Agur, King Lemuel, and the scribes of King Hezekiah who copied out Solomon's sayings. Many scholars think the book grew over several centuries, gathered by royal scribes and teachers, with its final form perhaps after the Jews returned from exile in Babylon.

**Who first heard it.** Much of the book speaks to a 'son,' likely young men being trained for adult life, whether by parents at home or by teachers preparing future officials at the royal court. Over time it became shared teaching for ordinary Israelite households, guiding everyday choices in work, family, money and speech.

**What was happening.** The Bible remembers Solomon's reign as a rare time of peace and wealth for Israel, with busy foreign trade, a splendid court and the building of the first Temple in Jerusalem, though historians and archaeologists debate how large and grand his kingdom really was. Neighbouring lands such as Egypt and Mesopotamia had their own long traditions of wise sayings, often used to train young officials, and Israel's teachers shared in this wider world. Later collecting happened under King Hezekiah around 700 BC, when the small kingdom of Judah lived under the shadow of the mighty Assyrian empire.

### Ecclesiastes

**Who wrote it.** The book names its writer as 'the Teacher' (in Hebrew, Qoheleth, a word linked to gathering, probably someone who gathers people to teach them), a son of David and king in Jerusalem. Jewish and Christian tradition has long identified him as King Solomon, famed for his wisdom and wealth. Many scholars think it was written later by a wise teacher who spoke in Solomon's voice, partly because its Hebrew resembles later writings. Either way, the writer belonged to Israel's tradition of wise men who taught through sayings and reflections on life.

**Who first heard it.** It was written for Israelites who wanted to think carefully about work, wealth, pleasure, injustice and death. If written by Solomon, its first readers lived in Israel's prosperous golden age. If written later, they were Jews in the small province of Judah, living under foreign rulers and dealing with taxes, officials and trade.

**What was happening.** On the traditional view, the setting is Solomon's reign, around 970–930 BC, a time of peace, great building projects, international trade and royal splendour in Jerusalem. On the later view, it comes from around 450–250 BC, when Judah was ruled by the Persian Empire and then by Greek kings after Alexander the Great. Throughout this whole period, nations across the Middle East had their own traditions of wisdom writing about how to live well.

### Song of Solomon

**Who wrote it.** The opening line links the book to Solomon, king of Israel around 970–930 BC, the son of King David, remembered for his wisdom and said to have written over a thousand songs. The Hebrew wording can mean a song by Solomon, for him, or about him, so readers have long weighed how directly he wrote it. Traditionally Solomon is the author. Many scholars think the poems were written or gathered later by unnamed poets, perhaps over a long period, partly because of features in the Hebrew language.

**Who first heard it.** No one knows exactly who first heard these poems. They may have been enjoyed at a royal court, among ordinary people, or both. Whoever they were, they knew vineyards, flocks, gardens and wild hill country, the world the poems are full of. The songs may have been sung at weddings and celebrations, and later Jewish communities read the whole book aloud each year during Passover, the spring festival remembering the escape from Egypt.

**What was happening.** If written in Solomon's day, it comes from Israel's golden age: peace, wealth, the building of the first temple in Jerusalem, and trade with Egypt, Phoenicia and Arabia. Love poetry was well known across the ancient Middle East, and Egyptian love songs from around 1300–1100 BC use similar images. The poems name real places across the land, from Jerusalem and En-gedi to Lebanon, Gilead and Carmel.

### Isaiah

**Who wrote it.** Isaiah son of Amoz was a prophet in Jerusalem, active for about forty years, from around 740 to 700 BC, under Kings Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. He moved in royal circles and spoke directly to kings. He was married, and he gave his sons names that carried messages. Tradition credits him with the whole book; many scholars think chapters 40–66 were added by later prophets in his tradition, writing during and after the exile in Babylon.

**Who first heard it.** The first hearers were the people of Jerusalem and Judah, including their kings and leaders. They lived under constant threat from Assyria and were tempted to trust in foreign alliances. Many scholars think chapters 40–66 were written for a later situation: Jews exiled in Babylon after Jerusalem's destruction, and then those returning to rebuild a ruined city. Traditionally, these chapters are read as Isaiah's own words, looking ahead to that time.

**What was happening.** Isaiah lived as Assyria, a ruthless empire based in what is now northern Iraq, pushed west. In 722 BC Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel, and in 701 BC its king Sennacherib overran Judah and besieged Jerusalem. Judah's kings wavered between submitting to Assyria and seeking help from Egypt. The later chapters look to Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 587 BC, the exile that followed, and the rise of Cyrus of Persia, who let the exiles return from 538 BC.

### Jeremiah

**Who wrote it.** Jeremiah was the son of Hilkiah, from a family of priests in Anathoth, a small village a few miles north of Jerusalem. He was called as a prophet, someone who spoke God's messages to the people, while still young, and kept speaking for about forty years, often at great personal cost. His call is traditionally dated to around 627 BC, though some scholars think that date marks his birth and that his public work began nearer 609 BC. His friend and scribe Baruch wrote down many of his words. Traditionally Jeremiah is seen as the book's source; many scholars think his followers later collected and arranged it, noting that the ancient Greek version is shorter and ordered differently.

**Who first heard it.** Jeremiah spoke first to the kings, priests and ordinary people of Judah and its capital, Jerusalem, a small kingdom caught between powerful empires. Many of them refused to listen, and some tried to silence him. Later parts address those deported to Babylon and the refugees who fled to Egypt after Jerusalem fell.

**What was happening.** Jeremiah lived through the collapse of the Assyrian Empire and the rise of Babylon. Good King Josiah led a sweeping religious reform around 622 BC but was killed in battle by Egypt's Pharaoh Necho in 609 BC. After Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605 BC, Judah became its subject, and repeated rebellions led to a first deportation of Jerusalem's leaders in 597 BC. In 587 or 586 BC the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem and its temple and carried many more into exile.

### Lamentations

**Who wrote it.** The book itself names no author. Jewish and Christian tradition has long credited it to Jeremiah, a prophet who lived through the fall of Jerusalem and had warned of it for years. Many scholars instead think it came from one or more unnamed poets in Judah who saw the disaster firsthand. Either way, the writer was a skilled poet, shaping most chapters as alphabet poems in which each verse or group of verses begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet.

**Who first heard it.** The first hearers were survivors of Jerusalem's destruction, mostly people left behind among the ruins of Judah, and perhaps also those carried off to Babylon. Their city lay burned, their temple was gone, and many had lost family to war, famine and exile. The poems were likely recited at gatherings where people mourned the ruined temple, and Jewish communities still read them each year on a fast day remembering its destruction.

**What was happening.** Around 588 BC the armies of Nebuchadnezzar, king of the powerful Babylonian empire, surrounded Jerusalem after Judah rebelled. The siege brought terrible famine, and in 587 or 586 BC the walls were broken, the temple Solomon had built was burned, and King Zedekiah was captured. Many leading citizens were marched into exile in Babylon, while neighbouring peoples looked on and some joined in the plunder.

### Ezekiel

**Who wrote it.** The book is traditionally credited to Ezekiel, the son of Buzi, a priest from Jerusalem trained to serve in the temple. He was taken to Babylon as a captive in 597 BC, along with King Jehoiachin and many of Judah's leading families. He lived among the exiles in a settlement called Tel Abib (the modern Israeli city of Tel Aviv later took its name from it), beside the Kebar canal. There, around 593 BC, he was called to be a prophet; most scholars think much of the book comes from him, though some believe followers later arranged and expanded it.

**Who first heard it.** The first hearers were Judeans living as exiles (people forced from their homeland) in Babylon, far from Jerusalem and its temple. Many hoped for a quick return home and believed Jerusalem could never fall. After the city was destroyed in 586 BC, they were left grieving the loss of their city, their king and their temple.

**What was happening.** The Babylonian empire under King Nebuchadnezzar dominated the region, and Judah was a subject kingdom paying tribute. After a first deportation in 597 BC, Babylon placed Zedekiah on Judah's throne, but he rebelled, hoping Egypt would help. Babylon besieged Jerusalem from about 588 BC and destroyed the city and Solomon's temple in 586 BC. Ezekiel's dated messages span roughly 593 to 571 BC.

### Daniel

**Who wrote it.** Traditionally the book is credited to Daniel, a young Jew from a noble or royal family who was taken from Jerusalem to Babylon as a teenager around 605 BC. He was trained to serve in the Babylonian court and rose to become a senior royal official and a trusted interpreter of dreams under both Babylonian and Persian kings. Chapters 1–6 tell stories about him in the third person, while chapters 7–12 record his visions in his own voice. Many modern scholars think the book reached its final form around 165 BC, when an unnamed faithful Jewish writer in Judea gathered older stories about Daniel and added the visions.

**Who first heard it.** On the traditional view, the first readers were Jews living in exile in Babylon and afterwards, serving under powerful foreign rulers far from home. On the later-date view, the book was written for Jews in Judea around 167–164 BC, when the Greek-Syrian king Antiochus IV outlawed their faith on pain of death. In both pictures, the readers were God's people living under powerful foreign empires.

**What was happening.** The stories span from around 605 BC, when the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar first took captives from Judah, through his destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in 586 BC, when many more of its people were carried off to Babylon, to the early years of Persian rule. In 539 BC Babylon fell to Cyrus of Persia, who soon allowed exiles to go home and rebuild. If the book was finished in the 160s BC, its writer lived under Greek rulers descended from Alexander the Great's generals, during Antiochus IV's persecution and the Jewish uprising led by the Maccabee family. In Jewish Bibles, Daniel sits among the Writings rather than the Prophets.

### Hosea

**Who wrote it.** The book is named after Hosea son of Beeri, a prophet: someone believed to speak messages from God to his people. He is the only prophet from the northern kingdom of Israel whose messages to his own people fill a book of the Bible. Little is known of his daily life beyond his troubled marriage to a woman named Gomer, which the book describes. Most take this as a real marriage, though some, both in church tradition and among scholars, have read it as a vision or symbolic story. Most agree the messages go back to Hosea himself, though many scholars think they were later collected and edited in the southern kingdom of Judah, with a few lines added for readers there.

**Who first heard it.** Hosea first spoke to the people of the northern kingdom of Israel, which he often calls 'Ephraim' after its leading tribe, and to its leaders in the capital, Samaria. Many mixed worship of Israel's God with worship of Baal, a Canaanite god believed to bring rain and good harvests, and the kingdom's official shrines held golden calf statues. After the north fell, the collected messages were treasured by readers in Judah to the south.

**What was happening.** Hosea was active from roughly 750 to 720 BC. He began during the long, prosperous reign of King Jeroboam II, but after Jeroboam's death the northern kingdom fell into chaos, with six kings in about 30 years, four of them murdered. The mighty Assyrian empire, based in what is now Iraq, was expanding westward, and Israel's rulers swung between paying it off and seeking help from Egypt. In 722 BC Assyria captured Samaria and carried many Israelites into exile, ending the northern kingdom.

### Joel

**Who wrote it.** The book names its author as Joel son of Pethuel, and nothing more is known about him or his family. He was a prophet, someone who spoke messages from God to the people. He knew Jerusalem, its temple and its priests well, which suggests he lived in or near the city, and some think he may have had close links with temple worship.

**Who first heard it.** Joel spoke to the people of Judah and Jerusalem, including the priests who led worship at the temple and the elders who led the community. They had just been struck by a devastating swarm of locusts and a drought that stripped the fields, vineyards and orchards bare. Food was scarce, and even the grain and wine used in daily temple offerings had run out.

**What was happening.** The date of Joel is unclear because the book names no king. Traditionally it has been placed early, around 800 BC, perhaps while the boy-king Joash ruled Judah under the guidance of priests. Many scholars today date it later, around 500–350 BC, after the Jews returned from exile in Babylon and rebuilt the temple, when Judah was a small province under Persian rule. Either way, Judah was a small land whose people lived mostly by farming and whose survival depended on rain and harvest, and locust swarms were a dreaded and familiar disaster.

### Amos

**Who wrote it.** The book is named after Amos, a man from Tekoa, a small village in the hill country of Judah, a few miles south of Bethlehem. He was not a professional prophet or a priest; he worked as a herdsman of sheep and also tended sycamore-fig trees. He travelled north to deliver his messages in the neighbouring kingdom of Israel. Most scholars accept that the core of the book comes from Amos himself, though many think some passages were added or arranged later by those who preserved his words.

**Who first heard it.** His first hearers were the people of the northern kingdom of Israel, especially the wealthy in the capital, Samaria, and worshippers at the royal shrine of Bethel. They were living through a time of peace and prosperity, busy with festivals and offerings, while a sharp gap had opened between rich landowners and the poor.

**What was happening.** Amos spoke around 760–750 BC, when Uzziah ruled Judah in the south and Jeroboam II ruled Israel in the north. With the great empire of Assyria (in what is now Iraq) temporarily weakened, Jeroboam had won back territory and trade flourished, bringing fine houses and luxury goods to the upper classes. Within a few decades Assyria grew strong again, and in 722 BC it destroyed Samaria and ended the northern kingdom.

### Obadiah

**Who wrote it.** The book is credited to Obadiah, whose name means 'servant of the Lord'. He was a prophet, someone who brought God's messages to the people, but nothing else is known about his family, hometown or life. Ancient Jewish tradition identified him with the Obadiah who served in King Ahab's household, though most scholars today think this is unlikely.

**Who first heard it.** The message is addressed to Edom, a neighbouring nation descended from Esau, Jacob's brother. Its real first hearers, however, were the people of Judah, stung by the way the Edomites had looked on and joined in when Jerusalem was attacked and plundered, at a time whose exact date is still debated.

**What was happening.** Most scholars connect the book to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Edom sided with the invaders and took advantage of Judah's collapse. Some instead date it earlier, to around 845 BC, when Philistines and Arabs raided Jerusalem during King Jehoram's reign. Either way, Israel and Edom had a long history of bitter rivalry stretching back centuries, despite their shared ancestry.

### Jonah

**Who wrote it.** The book does not name its writer. Traditionally it is linked to Jonah son of Amittai himself, a prophet from the village of Gath-hepher in Galilee who served in the northern kingdom of Israel under King Jeroboam II, around 780–750 BC. Many scholars think an unknown Israelite writer told Jonah's story much later, perhaps after the Jews returned from exile in Babylon, around 500–400 BC, partly because the story is told about Jonah rather than by him, and because of features of its Hebrew.

**Who first heard it.** On the traditional view, the first hearers were Israelites in the northern kingdom, who knew Assyria as a powerful and brutal empire to the north-east, whose armies had troubled the region before and would soon return. If the book was written later, its readers were Jews rebuilding their life in and around Jerusalem after the exile, under Persian rule and living alongside many other peoples.

**What was happening.** In Jonah's day the northern kingdom of Israel was enjoying a rare stretch of peace and prosperity under Jeroboam II, regaining land it had lost. To the east lay Assyria, a great empire known for its brutal warfare, and Nineveh, on the River Tigris near modern Mosul in Iraq, was one of its greatest cities. A few decades later, in 722 BC, Assyria would conquer Israel and carry many of its people away.

### Micah

**Who wrote it.** The book is linked to Micah of Moresheth, a small town in the low hill country of Judah about 25 miles southwest of Jerusalem. He was a prophet, someone who brought messages from God to the people. He came from the countryside, not the royal court, and spoke up for small farmers. Traditionally Micah wrote the whole book; many scholars think his words were later gathered and expanded by followers, especially the hopeful passages in chapters 4–7.

**Who first heard it.** Micah's messages were first spoken to the people of Judah and its capital Jerusalem, and also to the northern kingdom of Israel and its capital Samaria. He spoke especially to rulers, judges, priests and prophets, and to wealthy landowners who were taking land and homes from poorer families.

**What was happening.** Micah prophesied around 740–700 BC, during the reigns of Judah's kings Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, at the same time as the prophet Isaiah. The Assyrian Empire, based in what is now northern Iraq, was conquering the region. Assyria destroyed Samaria in 722 BC, and in 701 BC its king Sennacherib invaded Judah, capturing many towns near Micah's home.

### Nahum

**Who wrote it.** The book is credited to Nahum, a prophet described only as coming from Elkosh, a village whose location is now unknown. Some suggest a site in Galilee or southern Judah, but nobody can be sure. A prophet was someone believed to carry messages from God to the people. Beyond his name and hometown, nothing else is known about his life or family.

**Who first heard it.** Nahum's words were first heard by the people of Judah, the small southern kingdom centred on Jerusalem. For generations they had lived under the shadow of Assyria, paying it heavy tribute and fearing its armies. Assyria had already wiped out the northern kingdom of Israel and once besieged Jerusalem itself.

**What was happening.** Assyria, ruled from its capital Nineveh, was the greatest and most feared empire of its day, known for brutal punishment of conquered peoples. The book mentions the Assyrian sack of the Egyptian city of Thebes in about 663 BC, and Nineveh itself fell in 612 BC, so most date Nahum between these two events. Some place him soon after Thebes fell, while others place him closer to Nineveh's collapse. Over those decades Assyria weakened, Babylon and the Medes rose against it, and in Judah the long rule of King Manasseh gave way to the reforms of King Josiah.

### Habakkuk

**Who wrote it.** The book is credited to Habakkuk, who is simply called 'the prophet', someone who received and passed on messages from God. Almost nothing is known about his family, hometown or life. Because the final chapter is a prayer set to music with instructions for a choir leader, many think he may have served among the prophets connected with worship at the temple in Jerusalem. Most scholars accept him as the source of the book, though some think the closing prayer was first a separate psalm that was later joined to it.

**Who first heard it.** His first hearers were the people of Judah, the small southern kingdom centred on Jerusalem, at a time when a new and frightening empire was rising in the east. Most readers take the book's opening complaint to describe life in Judah itself: violence, unfair courts and powerful people exploiting the weak. Some scholars think it instead describes the cruelty of a foreign oppressor such as Assyria or Babylon.

**What was happening.** Habakkuk is usually dated to the years around 600 BC, probably between about 609 and 597 BC. The old Assyrian empire had collapsed, its capital Nineveh falling in 612 BC, and Babylon was rising quickly, defeating Egypt at the battle of Carchemish in 605 BC. In Judah, good King Josiah had been killed in 609 BC, and his son Jehoiakim's rule is remembered as harsh and unjust. Within a few years Babylon would invade Judah, carrying off captives in 597 BC and destroying Jerusalem in 586 BC.

### Zephaniah

**Who wrote it.** Zephaniah was a prophet, someone who spoke messages he believed came from God, and he seems to have lived in or near Jerusalem. The book's opening line traces his family back four generations to a man named Hezekiah, possibly the well-known King Hezekiah, which would make Zephaniah a distant relative of the royal family, though this is not certain. Beyond this family line, almost nothing is known about his life.

**Who first heard it.** His first hearers were the people of Judah, the small southern kingdom of the Israelites, and especially the residents of its capital, Jerusalem. Many had drifted into worshipping other gods alongside the God of Israel, and the leaders and wealthy classes were comfortable and confident.

**What was happening.** The book places Zephaniah in the reign of King Josiah, who ruled Judah from about 640 to 609 BC, and many think he spoke before Josiah's major religious reforms, which reached their height around 622 BC. Before Josiah, King Manasseh's long reign had filled Judah with the worship of foreign gods. The great empire of Assyria, which had dominated the region for a century, was weakening, and Babylon was rising; Assyria's capital, Nineveh, fell in 612 BC.

### Haggai

**Who wrote it.** Haggai was a prophet, someone called to speak God's messages to the people, who lived in Jerusalem after the Jews returned from exile in Babylon. We know almost nothing about his family or background; outside this book he appears only briefly in the book of Ezra, alongside the prophet Zechariah. Traditionally Haggai is seen as the author of his own messages, though because the book speaks of him in the third person, many scholars think a follower or editor gathered and arranged his words soon after he spoke them.

**Who first heard it.** Haggai first spoke to the small community of Jews who had come back to Jerusalem and the surrounding land of Judah, along with their leaders, Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest (the chief priest). About sixteen years earlier they had begun rebuilding God's temple, but the work had stalled amid opposition from neighbours, poor harvests and the struggle to rebuild their own homes and lives.

**What was happening.** Haggai's messages are dated to 520 BC, in the second year of Darius I, king of the vast Persian Empire, who had seized the throne after a period of upheaval and revolts. Persia had conquered Babylon in 539 BC, and its king Cyrus allowed exiled Jews to return home and rebuild the temple that the Babylonians had destroyed in 586 BC. Judah was now a small, poor province within the Persian Empire, and Jerusalem still lay largely in ruins. The rebuilt temple was finished around 516 BC.

### Zechariah

**Who wrote it.** Zechariah was a prophet, a man who brought messages he believed came from God. He was the son of Berechiah and grandson of Iddo, and he likely came from a priestly family that had returned from exile in Babylon. He worked alongside the prophet Haggai, beginning around 520 BC. Chapters 1–8 are widely accepted as his, but many scholars think chapters 9–14 come from a later writer or writers, while others hold that Zechariah wrote the whole book.

**Who first heard it.** The first hearers were Jews who had come back to Jerusalem and Judah after decades of exile in Babylon. They lived in a small, poor community among ruins, and their work rebuilding God's temple had stalled for years amid hardship, opposition and discouragement.

**What was happening.** The Persian Empire ruled the region after defeating Babylon, and in 538 BC the Persian king Cyrus allowed exiled Jews to return home. Foundations for a new temple were laid, but work stalled until 520 BC. King Darius I seized the throne in 522 BC and spent his first years putting down revolts across the empire. Under the encouragement of Haggai and Zechariah the temple was finally completed around 516 BC.

### Malachi

**Who wrote it.** Almost nothing is known about the writer beyond the name Malachi, which means 'my messenger' in Hebrew. Tradition treats it as the personal name of a prophet, someone called to speak God's word to the people. Many scholars think it may instead be a title drawn from a phrase later in the book, leaving the prophet unnamed. Either way, he was clearly someone who knew the temple, its priests and its worship well.

**Who first heard it.** The book was first addressed to the Jewish community in and around Jerusalem, especially the priests who served at the rebuilt temple. They were a small, poor community under foreign rule, and many had grown tired and doubtful as the hopes stirred by their return from exile seemed to go unfulfilled.

**What was happening.** The book is usually dated to around 460 to 430 BC, when Judah was a minor province of the vast Persian Empire, ruled by a Persian-appointed governor. Jewish exiles had been allowed home from Babylon about a century earlier, and the temple had been rebuilt by around 516 BC. Worship had resumed, but enthusiasm had faded. The book is often placed close to the time of Ezra and Nehemiah, leaders who faced many of the same problems.

### Matthew

**Who wrote it.** Christians have traditionally credited this gospel to Matthew, also called Levi, a tax collector who left his booth to become one of Jesus' twelve apostles. Tax collectors gathered tolls for the ruling powers, often took extra for themselves, and were widely despised by fellow Jews. Many modern scholars think the book was written by an unnamed Jewish Christian teacher who drew on Mark's gospel and a collection of Jesus' sayings, with Matthew's name attached early on. Either way, the writer knew the Jewish Scriptures and customs closely.

**Who first heard it.** It was written mainly for Jewish followers of Jesus, perhaps in Syria around the city of Antioch or in Galilee, along with non-Jews joining their churches. They were working out how faith in Jesus fitted with their Jewish heritage, often amid painful arguments with local synagogue leaders.

**What was happening.** Jesus lived under Roman rule, with local kings from Herod's family and Roman governors such as Pontius Pilate in charge. The gospel was most likely written around AD 70–90, though some date it to the 60s. In AD 66 the Jews revolted against Rome, and in AD 70 Roman armies destroyed Jerusalem and its temple. Afterwards, Jewish life regrouped largely around the synagogue and the teaching of the Pharisees.

### Mark

**Who wrote it.** The Gospel itself never names its writer, but from the early second century Christians credited it to Mark, traditionally identified with John Mark, a Jewish believer from Jerusalem whose mother's house was a meeting place for the first church. He travelled with his cousin Barnabas and with Paul, and later worked alongside Peter, and an early church leader named Papias said Mark wrote down Peter's memories of Jesus. Many scholars accept this tradition, while others think the author was an unknown Christian teacher. Most agree it was probably the first of the four Gospels to be written.

**Who first heard it.** Early tradition says Mark wrote for Christians in Rome, many of them not Jewish, which fits the way he explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic words, the everyday language Jesus spoke. These believers may have been living through, or just after, the fierce persecution under Emperor Nero around AD 64, when Peter is said to have died. Some scholars instead suggest a church in Syria or Galilee.

**What was happening.** Mark was probably written around AD 65–70, a frightening time when Jews in Judea rose up against Rome and the Roman army crushed the revolt, destroying Jerusalem's temple in AD 70. The events it describes took place about forty years earlier, around AD 30, in Galilee and Judea under Roman rule. Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, governed Galilee, while the Roman governor Pontius Pilate ruled Judea.

### Luke

**Who wrote it.** From early times the church has named Luke as the writer: a doctor and travelling companion of the apostle Paul, who mentions him in his letters. He was probably a Gentile (not Jewish), well educated and writing in polished Greek, and he also wrote the book of Acts as a second volume. He was not an eyewitness of Jesus' life but gathered accounts from those who were. Many accept this tradition, though others think the author was an unknown later Christian, perhaps connected with Paul's circle.

**Who first heard it.** Luke addresses his work to Theophilus, probably a well-placed patron or official, possibly a new believer. Behind him stood a wider circle of mostly Gentile Christians spread across the Roman Empire, many of whom had never seen Judea. They wanted a careful, orderly account of the faith they had joined.

**What was happening.** Jesus lived under Roman rule, when Judea and Galilee were governed by Roman officials and local kings from Herod's family. Luke wrote decades later, as the church was spreading from Jewish communities into Greek-speaking cities across the empire. Traditionally the book is dated to the early 60s AD, though many scholars place it around AD 80–90, after the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70.

### John

**Who wrote it.** Christians have traditionally held that this Gospel was written by John, son of Zebedee, a fisherman from Galilee who became one of Jesus' twelve closest followers. The book itself points to an unnamed 'disciple whom Jesus loved' as its eyewitness source. Many scholars think it was written down by a follower or a circle of followers of that disciple, or by another early Christian leader named John, drawing on his testimony.

**Who first heard it.** Early tradition places its first readers around Ephesus, a large city in what is now western Turkey, though this is not certain. They seem to have been a mix of Jewish and non-Jewish believers. The writer explains Jewish customs and translates Hebrew and Aramaic words, so not every reader knew them. Some may have been pushed out of their local synagogues for following Jesus.

**What was happening.** The events take place around AD 30, when Judea was under Roman rule through the governor Pontius Pilate and Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas. The Gospel was most likely written around AD 90, a generation later, though some suggest an earlier date. By then the Romans had destroyed Jerusalem and its temple in AD 70. Jewish communities were rebuilding without the temple, and tensions between synagogues and followers of Jesus were growing.

### Acts

**Who wrote it.** Acts is the second volume of a two-part work; the same writer produced the Gospel of Luke, and both are addressed to a man named Theophilus. Church tradition from the second century names the author as Luke, a doctor and travelling companion of the apostle Paul, probably a non-Jew with a good Greek education. Some passages switch from 'they' to 'we', which many take as the writer joining Paul's journeys. Many scholars accept Luke, while others think an unnamed later Christian wrote it, possibly drawing on a travel diary.

**Who first heard it.** The book is formally addressed to Theophilus, possibly a well-placed patron or official, or simply a respected believer who wanted a reliable account. Beyond him, it was meant for the young churches scattered around the Mediterranean, a mix of Jewish and non-Jewish believers working out who they were and how they related to Judaism and to Rome.

**What was happening.** The events run from about AD 30 to around AD 62, under Roman emperors from Tiberius to Nero, when good roads and shipping lanes linked cities across the empire. Jewish communities with synagogues were found in almost every major city, and Judea was ruled by Roman governors and members of the Herod family amid rising unrest. The book was most likely written somewhere between the early 60s and the 80s AD, though some scholars place it later.

### Romans

**Who wrote it.** The apostle Paul, one of the few New Testament authors whose authorship almost all scholars accept. He was a Jew from Tarsus, in what is now Turkey, trained as a Pharisee (a member of a strict Jewish group devoted to keeping God's law) and a tentmaker by trade. Once a fierce opponent of Jesus' followers, he became an apostle, a messenger sent to carry the news about Jesus, especially to Gentiles (non-Jewish people). He dictated this letter to a helper named Tertius, who wrote it down.

**Who first heard it.** Christians in Rome, the capital of the empire, meeting in small groups in people's homes, a mix of Jewish believers and a growing number of Gentile believers. Paul had not founded this church and had never visited it. Around AD 49 the emperor Claudius expelled Jews from Rome, and many returned only after his death in AD 54, which may have left Jewish and Gentile believers adjusting to one another.

**What was happening.** Paul probably wrote around AD 56–58, most likely from the Greek city of Corinth near the end of his travels around the eastern Mediterranean. The young emperor Nero had come to power, and his early years were seen as calm and well governed, with Roman roads and peace making travel easier. Paul was collecting money from Gentile churches to carry to poor believers in Jerusalem, and he hoped afterwards to visit Rome on his way to preach in Spain.

### 1 Corinthians

**Who wrote it.** The letter is from Paul, with a co-worker named Sosthenes joined in the greeting. Paul was a Jew from Tarsus, in what is now southern Turkey, trained as a Pharisee (a member of a Jewish group devoted to strictly keeping God's law). He once persecuted Christians, but after encountering the risen Jesus he became a travelling missionary, an apostle who started churches across the Roman world. He earned his living as a tentmaker, and almost all scholars agree he wrote this letter.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were the believers in Corinth, a church Paul had started around AD 50–52 during a stay of about eighteen months. Most were non-Jews, along with some Jews, and they met in one another's homes. A few years after Paul left, he heard reports of quarrels and troubles among them, and they had also written to him with questions.

**What was happening.** Paul wrote around AD 53–55, most likely from the city of Ephesus, in what is now western Turkey. Corinth was a busy Roman colony in southern Greece, rebuilt by Julius Caesar in 44 BC after an earlier destruction. It sat on a narrow strip of land between two seaports, so it was crowded with traders, sailors, slaves and freed slaves, and full of temples to many gods. The Christian movement was only about twenty-five years old.

### 2 Corinthians

**Who wrote it.** The letter comes from Paul, a Jewish man from Tarsus who had been a Pharisee, a member of a Jewish group devoted to strictly keeping God's law, before becoming a follower of Jesus. He worked as a travelling apostle, a missionary who started churches across the Roman world, and supported himself by making tents. He had founded the church in Corinth himself around AD 50–51. Nearly everyone accepts Paul wrote it, though many scholars think the book joins two or more of his letters to Corinth, while others read it as one letter.

**Who first heard it.** It was written to the church in Corinth, mostly non-Jewish converts with some Jewish believers, and to Christians across Achaia, the Roman province of southern Greece. Paul's relationship with them had become strained, with a painful visit, a sorrowful letter, and visiting rival teachers who questioned his authority.

**What was happening.** Paul probably wrote around AD 55–56 from Macedonia in northern Greece, during the early reign of the emperor Nero. Corinth was a wealthy, busy Roman colony and trading port, rebuilt by Julius Caesar a century earlier, full of temples, merchants and travelling speakers. Paul was also gathering money from his non-Jewish churches to help poor believers in Jerusalem.

### Galatians

**Who wrote it.** The letter comes from Paul, and almost all scholars agree he wrote it. He was a Jew from Tarsus, in what is now southern Turkey, trained as a Pharisee (a member of a Jewish group devoted to strictly keeping God's law), and once fiercely opposed to the followers of Jesus. After meeting the risen Jesus, he became an apostle, a messenger sent to spread the good news, travelling from city to city founding churches and often supporting himself as a tentmaker.

**Who first heard it.** It went to several churches in Galatia, a region in the middle of modern Turkey. Scholars disagree on which: traditionally the Celtic Galatian people in the north, though many think it means towns in the south such as Iconium, Lystra and Derbe, which Paul visited on his first journey. These were mostly non-Jewish believers, and new teachers had arrived insisting they be circumcised and keep the Jewish law to belong fully to God's people.

**What was happening.** Paul probably wrote around AD 48–55, while Rome ruled the whole Mediterranean world under Emperor Claudius and then, from AD 54, Nero. Some date it very early, before the church leaders met in Jerusalem to settle this question around AD 49; others date it a few years later. It was a time when the young church, which began among Jews, was working out whether non-Jewish believers had to follow Jewish customs.

### Ephesians

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Paul as its author. He was a Jewish teacher from Tarsus, trained as a Pharisee (a strict group devoted to God's law), who worked as a tentmaker and became a travelling apostle, or messenger, of Jesus. He writes as a prisoner, traditionally while under house arrest in Rome around AD 60–62. Many scholars note differences in style and vocabulary from his other letters and think a close follower wrote it in his name, perhaps around AD 80–90.

**Who first heard it.** The letter is addressed to Christians in Ephesus, a large port city in the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey, where Paul had spent about three years. Several of the oldest manuscripts leave out the words "in Ephesus," so many think it was meant to circulate among several churches in the region. The readers were mostly non-Jewish believers, worshipping alongside Jewish Christians in small house churches.

**What was happening.** The Roman Empire ruled the whole Mediterranean world, and if Paul wrote it, Nero was emperor. Ephesus was one of the empire's greatest cities, famous for its huge temple to the goddess Artemis, one of the wonders of the ancient world, and known for magic and fortune-telling. Early Christians were still working out how Jews and non-Jews, long divided by custom and religious law, could belong to one community.

### Philippians

**Who wrote it.** The apostle Paul wrote this letter, with his younger co-worker Timothy named alongside him. Paul was a Jewish teacher from Tarsus, trained as a Pharisee (a group devoted to strict keeping of God's law), who once persecuted Christians before becoming a follower of Jesus and a travelling missionary who planted churches around the Mediterranean. He wrote while a prisoner awaiting trial. Traditionally this was in Rome around AD 60–62, though some scholars suggest an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus (mid-50s) or Caesarea.

**Who first heard it.** The letter went to the church in Philippi, a city in Macedonia (northern Greece), which Paul had founded around AD 49–50; its first members included a businesswoman named Lydia and a local jailer. It was a small, mostly non-Jewish congregation that faced opposition from outsiders and had generously supported Paul, recently sending him a gift by a messenger named Epaphroditus.

**What was happening.** The Roman Empire ruled the Mediterranean world, with Nero as emperor at the likely time of writing. Philippi was a Roman colony, resettled with retired army veterans after a famous battle there in 42 BC, and its people were proud of their Roman citizenship and customs. Honouring the emperor was part of civic life, and a group that worshipped another Lord could draw suspicion. Prisoners like Paul often relied on friends to bring food and support while they waited, sometimes for years, for their cases to be heard.

### Colossians

**Who wrote it.** The letter opens in the name of Paul, a Jewish teacher and former persecutor of Christians who became a travelling missionary planting churches across the Roman world, with his young co-worker Timothy. Paul writes as a prisoner, traditionally in Rome around AD 60–62, though some suggest an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus. Many scholars think a close follower wrote it in Paul's name after his death, pointing to differences in style and emphasis; others see no reason to doubt Paul himself.

**Who first heard it.** It was written to a small church in Colossae, a town in the Lycus River valley in what is now western Turkey, near the larger cities of Laodicea and Hierapolis. Paul had never visited them, and most believers there seem to have been Gentiles (non-Jews) who had only recently turned to Christ from local religions.

**What was happening.** The Roman Empire ruled the region, with Nero as emperor from AD 54 to 68. Colossae had once been a busy wool-trading town but was being overshadowed by nearby Laodicea, and the valley was home to a mix of Greek, local Phrygian and Jewish communities. A powerful earthquake struck the area around AD 60. Christians were still a tiny, little-understood group meeting in homes.

### 1 Thessalonians

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Paul, along with his travelling companions Silvanus (also called Silas) and Timothy, and it is almost universally accepted as Paul's own. Paul was a Jew from Tarsus, in what is now southern Turkey, trained as a Pharisee, a member of a Jewish group devoted to strictly keeping God's law. He once persecuted followers of Jesus, then became one of the movement's leading missionaries, earning his living as a tentmaker as he travelled. This is probably one of his earliest surviving letters, and perhaps the oldest writing in the New Testament.

**Who first heard it.** It was written to a young church in Thessalonica, a busy port city and the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia in northern Greece. Most believers there seem to have been non-Jews who had recently turned away from worshipping idols. Paul had been forced to leave the city after only a short stay because of opposition, and the new believers were facing pressure and hostility from their neighbours.

**What was happening.** The letter was probably written around AD 50–51, during the reign of the emperor Claudius, likely from Corinth in southern Greece. Thessalonica sat on the Via Egnatia, a major Roman road, and was full of traders, temples to many gods, and honours paid to the emperor. Small groups meeting in homes to worship Jesus stood out in such a city and could easily draw suspicion.

### 2 Thessalonians

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Paul as its writer, together with his travelling companions Silas (also called Silvanus) and Timothy. Paul was a Jewish teacher from Tarsus, trained as a Pharisee (a strict keeper of Jewish law), who earned his living as a tentmaker and became a travelling missionary after coming to believe in Jesus. Traditionally the letter is accepted as Paul's own, written soon after 1 Thessalonians, though many scholars think a later follower wrote it in his name.

**Who first heard it.** It was written to a young church in Thessalonica, a busy port and the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia in northern Greece. Most members were non-Jews who had turned from local gods, and Paul had been forced to leave the city after only a short stay. The believers faced hostility from their neighbours and were unsettled by questions about the future.

**What was happening.** If Paul wrote it, the date is around AD 50–51, probably from Corinth in southern Greece, during the reign of the emperor Claudius. Thessalonica sat on the Via Egnatia, a major Roman road linking east and west, and was a self-governing city loyal to Rome that honoured the emperor alongside its traditional gods. Small groups of Jesus-followers met in homes and often drew suspicion for abandoning the city's religious customs.

### 1 Timothy

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Paul as its writer. He was a Jewish scholar trained as a Pharisee (a member of a Jewish group devoted to strictly keeping God's law), a tentmaker by trade, and a former persecutor of Christians who became a travelling missionary and founder of churches. Traditionally it is dated late in his life, perhaps in the early-to-mid 60s AD after a release from house arrest in Rome; many scholars instead think a later follower wrote it in Paul's name to pass on his teaching.

**Who first heard it.** It is addressed to Timothy, Paul's younger co-worker and close friend of many years, the son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, whom Paul had left in Ephesus to guide the church there. The believers met in homes, and some teachers among them were spreading confusing ideas and stirring up arguments, so Timothy needed help organising leadership and church life.

**What was happening.** Ephesus was a busy port and one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire, in what is now western Turkey, famous for its huge temple to the goddess Artemis. If Paul wrote the letter, Nero was emperor and Christians were a small, often misunderstood group; if it came later, it probably belongs to the late first or early second century AD. Society was built around households that included family members and slaves, and wealth and status mattered greatly in city life.

### 2 Timothy

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Paul as its writer, a Jewish teacher trained as a Pharisee (a strict keeper of Jewish law) and a tentmaker by trade, who became a travelling missionary and founded churches across the Roman world. Traditionally it is his last surviving letter, written from prison in Rome. Many scholars instead think a later follower wrote it in Paul's name, drawing on his memory and teaching, though many others hold to Paul himself.

**Who first heard it.** It is addressed to Timothy, Paul's younger co-worker for many years, the son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, apparently leading the church at Ephesus at the time. He faced rival teachers stirring up arguments, believers drifting away, and the danger that came with being linked to an imprisoned leader.

**What was happening.** If written by Paul, it dates to around AD 64–67, under the emperor Nero, who blamed Christians for the great fire of Rome in AD 64 and had many put to death. Christianity was still a small, unofficial movement with no legal protection. Ephesus, a great port city in what is now western Turkey, was famous for its huge temple to the goddess Artemis. Those who think a follower wrote it usually place it some decades later.

### Titus

**Who wrote it.** The letter opens in the name of Paul, a Jewish teacher from Tarsus (in modern Turkey) who was trained in the law, earned a living as a tentmaker, and became a travelling missionary who started churches across the Roman world. It is addressed to Titus, a non-Jewish (Greek) believer who had worked alongside Paul for years. Traditionally Paul wrote it himself; many scholars think a later follower wrote it in his name, together with 1 and 2 Timothy, which are similar letters known as the Pastoral Letters.

**Who first heard it.** The letter went first to Titus, whom Paul had left on the island of Crete to organise young churches in its towns and appoint leaders for them. These small groups of believers, meeting in homes, were being unsettled by rival teachers, and Titus was meant to share the letter's instructions with them.

**What was happening.** Crete, a large island south of Greece, had been a Roman province since 67 BC, and ancient writers gave its people a reputation for lying and unruliness. If Paul wrote the letter, it likely dates to the early or mid 60s AD, under the emperor Nero, after Paul's first imprisonment in Rome; those who see it as a later work place it somewhere between the late first century and the early second century. Christian groups were still small, met in private households, and were often viewed with suspicion by their neighbours.

### Philemon

**Who wrote it.** The letter comes from the apostle Paul, a Jewish teacher who had once persecuted Christians before becoming one of the best-known travelling missionaries of the early church. Almost all scholars agree that Paul wrote it. He wrote while held as a prisoner, probably under guard and awaiting trial. His younger co-worker Timothy is named alongside him.

**Who first heard it.** The letter is addressed mainly to Philemon, a believer of some means, most likely living in Colossae, a small town in what is now western Turkey. It concerns Onesimus, a slave from Philemon's household who had left him, met Paul, and become a Christian. Most think Onesimus had run away, though some suggest he went to Paul for help in a dispute with his master.

**What was happening.** This was the Roman Empire of the mid-first century AD, where slavery was an ordinary part of life and a runaway slave could face harsh punishment. Christians were still a small movement with no church buildings, gathering in private homes. Traditionally Paul wrote from house arrest in Rome around AD 60–62, under the emperor Nero. Some scholars suggest an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus, nearer Colossae, in the mid-50s.

### Hebrews

**Who wrote it.** No one knows for certain who wrote Hebrews, because the writer never gives a name. Early Christians in the East often credited it to the apostle Paul. Many scholars today doubt this because the style and vocabulary differ from his letters, and other suggestions include Paul's companions Barnabas or Apollos, or the teacher Priscilla. What is clear is that the writer was a skilled teacher and preacher, wrote some of the most polished Greek in the New Testament, knew the Jewish scriptures deeply, and was a friend of Timothy.

**Who first heard it.** The first readers were a particular community of Christians the writer knew well. They are traditionally thought to be Jewish believers, though some scholars think the group was mixed. They had once stood firm through public insults, the imprisonment of friends and the seizure of property, but were now weary and tempted to drift away. A closing greeting from believers from Italy leads many to link them with Rome.

**What was happening.** The letter was written within the Roman Empire, probably in the AD 60s, while the Jerusalem temple and its sacrifices still stood. Some scholars date it later, to the AD 80s, but it was already known when a church leader in Rome drew heavily on it in a letter of his own, usually dated to around AD 96. Christians were increasingly seen as separate from Judaism and therefore lost some of the protection Jewish communities enjoyed. In Rome, Emperor Claudius had expelled Jews around AD 49, and Emperor Nero turned violently on Christians after the great fire of AD 64.

### James

**Who wrote it.** Traditionally, this letter was written by James, a brother of Jesus, who did not follow him during his ministry but became a believer after the resurrection. He went on to lead the church in Jerusalem and was respected among Jewish believers for his devotion to God's law. He was killed in Jerusalem around AD 62. Some scholars think a later follower wrote in his name to honour his teaching. Others defend the traditional view.

**Who first heard it.** The letter is addressed to God's people scattered across the world. Traditionally this is taken to mean Jewish believers in Jesus living outside their homeland across the Roman world; some scholars think it describes all Christians, Jewish and Gentile alike, as God's people spread abroad. Many seem to have been poor, facing hardship and mistreatment, and sometimes pushed around by wealthy landowners and powerful neighbours.

**What was happening.** If James wrote it, the letter likely dates from the 40s or 50s AD, making it one of the earliest Christian writings. Some scholars date it later in the first century. Judea and the surrounding lands were under Roman rule, with rising tension between Jews and their Roman governors. In many places a small group of rich landowners held most of the land, while ordinary people worked as day labourers or small farmers.

### 1 Peter

**Who wrote it.** The letter names Simon Peter as its writer. He was a fisherman from Galilee, in northern Israel, who became one of Jesus' twelve closest disciples and later a leading figure in the early church. Many scholars instead think a follower of Peter wrote it in his name after his death, drawing on his teaching. The letter also mentions Silvanus, who may have helped write or carry it.

**Who first heard it.** The letter is addressed to Christians scattered across five regions of what is now Turkey, then part of the Roman Empire. Many seem to have been non-Jews who had left their old religious and social customs behind. Because of this, neighbours treated them with suspicion, slander and hostility, and they lived like outsiders in their own towns.

**What was happening.** The Roman Empire ruled the whole Mediterranean world, and everyday life was bound up with honouring the emperor and the local gods. Christians who refused to take part could be seen as odd, disloyal or even dangerous. Tradition dates the letter to the early 60s AD, shortly before Peter was put to death in Rome under the emperor Nero; many scholars prefer a date between about AD 70 and 90.

### 2 Peter

**Who wrote it.** The letter names its writer as Simon Peter, a fisherman from Galilee whom Jesus called to be one of his twelve closest followers, known as apostles. Peter became a leading figure in the early church, preaching in Jerusalem and travelling widely. The letter presents itself as written near the end of his life. Many scholars think a follower wrote it in Peter's name after his death, because its style differs from 1 Peter; the traditional view holds that Peter wrote it himself, perhaps with a helper.

**Who first heard it.** It was written to Christians who had already received an earlier letter from Peter, probably the believers in what is now Turkey addressed in 1 Peter. Teachers had arisen among them who mocked the hope that Jesus would return and encouraged careless, self-indulgent living.

**What was happening.** The Roman Empire ruled the Mediterranean world, and Greek language and ideas shaped daily life in its cities. If Peter wrote the letter, it dates to the mid-60s AD under Emperor Nero, shortly before Peter's death in Rome, where tradition says he was crucified. Those who see it as written later place it near the end of the first century or early in the second. Either way, the first generation of Jesus' followers was dying, and some Christians wondered why he had not yet come back.

### 1 John

**Who wrote it.** The letter never names its writer, but from the second century Christians have traditionally credited it to John the apostle, a Galilean fisherman and son of Zebedee who became one of Jesus' closest disciples. Early tradition says John spent his old age leading churches around Ephesus, in what is now western Turkey. Many scholars instead think it was written by a respected leader in a circle of churches shaped by John's teaching, possibly the figure later known as 'John the Elder.' Whoever he was, he writes as an older, trusted pastor who claims a close link to the first eyewitnesses of Jesus.

**Who first heard it.** It was most likely sent to a group of house churches, probably in or around Ephesus in the Roman province called Asia (a region on the western edge of modern Turkey, not the continent), whose members the writer knew well and cared for. These congregations had recently been shaken by a painful split over what to believe about Jesus, and those who remained were unsettled and needed reassurance.

**What was happening.** It is usually dated to around AD 90–100, though some place it a little earlier. The Roman Empire was at peace and its cities were full of temples to many gods, while the Jerusalem temple had been destroyed in AD 70, some twenty to thirty years earlier, and most of the original apostles had died. Christianity was spreading in Greek-speaking cities, and some teachers there were blending the faith with popular ideas that the physical world was inferior to the spiritual.

### 2 John

**Who wrote it.** The writer calls himself simply "the elder," a title for a respected senior leader who cared for a group of churches. Church tradition identifies him as John the apostle, a former fisherman from Galilee who became one of Jesus' closest followers and, in old age, a leader of churches around Ephesus. Many scholars instead think he was a different John, known as John the Elder, a leader in the same region. Either way, the letter shares its language and concerns with 1 John and 3 John.

**Who first heard it.** The letter is addressed to "the chosen lady and her children." Most think this is a warm way of naming a particular local church and its members, though some think it was a real woman and her family who hosted believers in her home. The church faced travelling teachers whose ideas about Jesus differed from what the elder had taught.

**What was happening.** It was likely written in the late first century AD, perhaps around AD 90, when the Roman Empire ruled the Mediterranean world and Christians were a small, scattered minority. Churches met in private homes, and travelling preachers moved from town to town, relying on believers to give them food and lodging. Some of these teachers claimed that Jesus had not truly come as a flesh-and-blood human, an idea that was dividing congregations in the region.

### 3 John

**Who wrote it.** The writer calls himself simply "the elder," a title for a respected older leader who cared for a group of churches. Since early times Christians have identified him as John, one of Jesus' twelve disciples and a former fisherman from Galilee, who is said to have spent his later years in Ephesus. Some scholars think the elder was a different leader, sometimes called John the Elder, who belonged to the same circle of churches. The letter shares its style and outlook with 1 and 2 John, and most readers see one hand behind all three.

**Who first heard it.** This is a short personal letter to one man, Gaius, a believer the elder loved and trusted. He was part of a local church, probably in the Roman province of Asia (western Turkey), that met in a home. The elder had heard good reports of how Gaius welcomed and cared for visiting Christian workers.

**What was happening.** The letter is usually dated to the late first century AD, perhaps around AD 90, when the Roman Empire ruled the Mediterranean world. Most churches were small groups meeting in private houses, and travelling preachers and teachers moved between them. With no church buildings or salaries, these travellers depended on believers to give them food, lodging and help for the next stage of their journey. Letters were carried by hand, often by a trusted traveller who could add news in person.

### Jude

**Who wrote it.** The writer calls himself Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James, usually taken to be the James who led the church in Jerusalem and was himself a brother of Jesus. Traditionally Jude is identified as one of the brothers of Jesus named in the Gospels. Jesus' brothers did not believe in him during his ministry, but they are found among the believers after the resurrection, and Paul mentions the Lord's brothers travelling as missionaries. Beyond this, little is known about Jude himself. Many scholars accept the traditional view, while others think a later follower wrote in Jude's name to carry his authority.

**Who first heard it.** The letter does not name its readers. They were probably believers steeped in Jewish Scripture and tradition, perhaps in Palestine or nearby, meeting in small house churches. Certain people had slipped into their gatherings, sharing their meals while living immorally and treating God's grace as a licence for it, and Jude writes urgently to warn the believers about them.

**What was happening.** Scholars date Jude anywhere from around AD 50–70, if Jude himself wrote it, to around AD 90 or a little later, if it was written in his name. The young church was spreading across the Roman Empire through small household congregations, often visited by travelling teachers whose claims were hard to check. Letters like this one were carried by messengers and read aloud when believers gathered.

### Revelation

**Who wrote it.** The writer calls himself John, a servant of Jesus who shared in the hardships of the believers he wrote to. Christian tradition from the second century identifies him as the apostle John, a former Galilean fisherman and one of Jesus' twelve closest followers, who later led churches around Ephesus. Many scholars think he was a different John, a respected Christian prophet in the same region, noting that his Greek style differs from John's Gospel. He wrote in a vivid style full of visions and symbols, drawing heavily on Old Testament prophets like Daniel and Ezekiel.

**Who first heard it.** It was first sent to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey, and was meant to be read aloud to each congregation. These were small groups of believers in busy, prosperous cities, some facing hostility, poverty or prison, others growing comfortable and blending in with the surrounding culture.

**What was happening.** Most scholars date the book to around AD 95, under the Roman emperor Domitian; some place it earlier, in the late 60s, after Emperor Nero's persecution of Christians in Rome. Rome ruled the whole Mediterranean world, and cities in Asia competed to honour the emperor with temples and festivals. Christians who refused to take part in emperor worship or in trade-guild feasts dedicated to pagan gods could face suspicion, lost business or worse. If the book was written around AD 95, Jerusalem's temple had already been destroyed by Roman armies in AD 70; on the earlier date, the war with Rome that led to its destruction was under way or about to begin.
